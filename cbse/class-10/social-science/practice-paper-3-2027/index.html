Section A: History

  1. Question 1 · 1 mark · History: The Rise of Nationalism in Europe
    Which of the following statements about the Zollverein are correct?
    a. It was a customs union formed in 1834 at the initiative of Prussia.
    b. It abolished tariff barriers among the states that joined it.
    c. It increased the number of currencies in the German states from two to over thirty.
    d. Most of the German states joined it.
    Choose the correct option:

    1. a and c only
    2. b and c only
    3. a, c and d
    4. a, b and d
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) a, b and d

    In 1834 a customs union or Zollverein was formed at the initiative of Prussia and was joined by most of the German states. It abolished tariff barriers and reduced the number of currencies from over thirty to two, so statement c reverses the change.

  2. Question 2 · 1 mark · History: Nationalism in India
    Why did Indians oppose the Rowlatt Act passed in 1919?

    1. It introduced separate electorates for different communities.
    2. It gave the government enormous powers to repress political activities and allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years.
    3. It imposed a tax on salt and gave the government a monopoly over its production.
    4. It closed large forest areas to villagers and stopped them from grazing their cattle.
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) It gave the government enormous powers to repress political activities and allowed detention of political prisoners without trial for two years.

    The Rowlatt Act was hurriedly passed despite the united opposition of the Indian members of the Imperial Legislative Council. It gave the government enormous powers to repress political activities and allowed political prisoners to be detained without trial for two years, so Gandhiji launched a nationwide satyagraha against it.

  3. Question 3 · 1 mark · History: The Making of a Global World
    Read the statements given below and choose the correct answer:
    Statement I: As early as 3000 BCE, an active coastal trade linked the Indus valley civilisations with present-day West Asia.
    Statement II: Cowries from the Maldives, used as a form of currency, found their way to China and East Africa for more than a millennium.

    1. Statement I is correct and II is incorrect
    2. Statement I is incorrect and II is correct
    3. Both I and II are correct
    4. Both I and II are incorrect
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) Both I and II are correct

    Both statements describe the links of the pre-modern world: coastal trade between the Indus valley and West Asia from about 3000 BCE, and cowries (seashells used as currency) from the Maldives reaching China and East Africa.

  4. Question 4 · 1 mark · History: Print Culture and the Modern World
    Which of the following statements about Johann Gutenberg and his printing press are correct?
    a. The olive press provided the model for his printing press.
    b. The first book he printed was the Bible.
    c. His printed books at first looked very different from handwritten manuscripts.
    d. About 180 copies of his first book were printed, and it took three years to produce them.
    Choose the correct option:

    1. a, b and d
    2. a, b and c
    3. b, c and d
    4. a and c only
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) a, b and d

    Gutenberg adapted the olive press and used moulds to cast metal type. By 1448 he had perfected the system; his first book was the Bible, of which about 180 copies were printed in three years. Printed books at first closely resembled manuscripts: the metal letters imitated handwritten styles, so c is wrong.

  5. Question 5 · 2 marks · History: The Making of a Global World
    Until the nineteenth century, thousands of people left Europe for America. Explain two reasons for this.

    2. Religious conflicts were common and religious dissenters were persecuted, so many fled to America, where by the eighteenth century plantations worked by slaves captured in Africa were growing cotton and sugar for European markets.

  6. Question 6 · 3 marks · History: The Rise of Nationalism in Europe
    Explain how culture played an important role in creating the idea of the nation in nineteenth-century Europe.

    OR Explain why the Balkans became a source of nationalist tension in Europe after 1871.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Romanticism, a cultural movement, criticised the glorification of reason and science and focused instead on emotions, intuition and mystical feelings. Romantic artists and poets tried to create a sense of a shared collective heritage and a common cultural past as the basis of a nation.
    2. The German philosopher Johann Gottfried Herder claimed that true German culture was to be discovered among the common people (das volk). Folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances were collected and recorded to carry the modern nationalist message to large audiences, many of whom were not literate.
    3. Language was used to develop national feeling. In Poland, which had been partitioned, the Russian language was imposed and Polish was forced out of schools; the clergy used Polish in church gatherings and religious instruction, so the use of Polish became a symbol of the struggle against Russian dominance.

    Marking scheme:

    • Romanticism: emotions and intuition; a shared collective heritage and common cultural past (1 mark)
    • Folk culture: Herder and das volk; folk songs, poetry and dances used to spread the nationalist message (1 mark)
    • Language: e.g. Polish used in church and religious instruction as a weapon against Russian dominance (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: 1. The Balkans was a region of great geographical and ethnic variation (modern-day Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia and Montenegro), whose inhabitants were broadly known as the Slavs. A large part of it was under the control of the Ottoman Empire.
    2. The spread of romantic nationalism and the disintegration of the Ottoman Empire made the region very explosive. The Balkan states, which gained independence one by one, were fiercely jealous of each other and each hoped to gain more territory at the expense of the others.
    3. The big European powers, such as Russia, Germany, England and Austro-Hungary, competed for trade, colonies, naval and military might, and each wanted to extend its control over the region. This led to a series of wars and finally to the First World War.

    Marking scheme:

    • Region of geographical and ethnic variation (Slav peoples), largely under the Ottoman Empire (1 mark)
    • Romantic nationalism and Ottoman disintegration; Balkan states jealous of each other, each seeking more territory (1 mark)
    • Rivalry among the big European powers over the region; series of wars leading to the First World War (1 mark)

  7. Question 7 · 5 marks · History: Nationalism in India
    Not all social groups responded to the call of Civil Disobedience in the same way. Explain why the participation of the dalits ('untouchables') and of many Muslims in the movement was limited. (3 + 2 = 5)

    OR Explain how a sense of collective belonging was developed among Indians during the national movement. Give five points.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Dalits:
    1. For long the Congress had ignored the dalits for fear of offending the sanatanis, the conservative high-caste Hindus. Gandhiji called them harijans and worked for temple entry and access to public wells and schools, but many dalit leaders wanted a different, political solution.
    2. They demanded reserved seats in educational institutions and a separate electorate that would choose dalit members for legislative councils. Dr B.R. Ambedkar organised the dalits into the Depressed Classes Association in 1930.
    3. Ambedkar clashed with Gandhiji at the second Round Table Conference by demanding separate electorates for dalits. When the British government conceded the demand, Gandhiji began a fast unto death; the Poona Pact of September 1932 gave the Depressed Classes reserved seats in the provincial and central legislative councils, but they were to be voted in by the general electorate. Dalit participation in the movement therefore remained limited, particularly in Maharashtra and Nagpur, where their organisation was strong.
    Muslims:
    4. After the decline of the Non-Cooperation-Khilafat movement, a large section of Muslims felt alienated from the Congress. From the mid-1920s the Congress came to be seen as more visibly associated with openly Hindu religious nationalist groups like the Hindu Mahasabha, and relations between the communities worsened.
    5. Efforts to agree on the question of representation in the future assemblies failed; at the All Parties Conference in 1928, M.R. Jayakar of the Hindu Mahasabha strongly opposed a compromise. Many Muslims were anxious about their status as a minority, so their response to the call for a united struggle was lukewarm.

    Marking scheme:

    • Dalits: Congress had long ignored them for fear of offending conservative high-caste Hindus (sanatanis) (1 mark)
    • Dalits: demand for reserved seats and separate electorates; Ambedkar's Depressed Classes Association (1930) (1 mark)
    • Dalits: clash at the second Round Table Conference and the Poona Pact (1932); limited participation (1 mark)
    • Muslims: alienation after the decline of Non-Cooperation-Khilafat; Congress seen as close to the Hindu Mahasabha (1 mark)
    • Muslims: failure of the talks on representation (All Parties Conference, 1928); anxiety about minority status (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: 1. Image of Bharat Mata: the identity of India came to be visually associated with Bharat Mata. Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay wrote 'Vande Mataram' as a hymn to the motherland; it was included in his novel Anandamath and widely sung during the Swadeshi movement. Abanindranath Tagore painted Bharat Mata as a calm, composed, divine and spiritual figure.
    2. Folklore: nationalists recorded folk tales sung by bards and toured villages to gather folk songs and legends, which they saw as the true picture of traditional culture. Rabindranath Tagore collected ballads, nursery rhymes and myths; in Madras, Natesa Sastri published a collection of Tamil folk tales, The Folklore of Southern India.
    3. Flags: during the Swadeshi movement in Bengal a tricolour flag (red, green and yellow) was designed with eight lotuses for the eight provinces and a crescent moon. By 1921 Gandhiji had designed the Swaraj flag, a tricolour (red, green and white) with a spinning wheel at the centre; carrying it in marches became a symbol of defiance.
    4. Reinterpretation of history: the British saw Indians as backward and primitive. Indian writers wrote about India's glorious past, when art, architecture, science, mathematics, religion, law, philosophy, crafts and trade had flourished, and urged readers to take pride in it.
    5. These symbols, songs and histories gave people of different regions and communities shared icons, so that many came to see themselves as part of the same nation.

    Marking scheme:

    • Image of Bharat Mata (Bankim Chandra's Vande Mataram / Abanindranath Tagore's painting) (1 mark)
    • Revival of folklore (e.g. Rabindranath Tagore; Natesa Sastri's The Folklore of Southern India) (1 mark)
    • Icons and symbols: Swadeshi flag (eight lotuses) / Gandhiji's Swaraj flag (1921) with spinning wheel (1 mark)
    • Reinterpretation of history: glorious developments in ancient India; pride in the past (1 mark)
    • Shared symbols united people of different regions and communities as one nation (1 mark)

  8. Question 8 · 4 marks · History: Print Culture and the Modern World
    Read the following text and answer the questions that follow:
    <b>Print and the Poor People</b>
    In nineteenth-century Madras towns, very cheap small books were brought to markets and sold at crossroads, so that poor people travelling to the markets could buy them. From the early twentieth century, public libraries were set up. They were located mostly in cities and towns, and at times in prosperous villages; for rich local patrons, setting up a library was a way of gaining prestige. From the late nineteenth century, issues of caste discrimination began to be written about in many printed tracts and essays. Jyotiba Phule, the Maratha pioneer of 'low caste' protest movements, wrote about the injustices of the caste system in a book published in 1871. Factory workers were usually too overworked, and lacked the education, to write much about their experiences. Even so, by the 1930s the cotton millworkers of Bangalore had set up libraries to educate themselves, following the example of workers in Bombay. These libraries were sponsored by social reformers.

    8.1 Name the book written by Jyotiba Phule about the injustices of the caste system. [1]

    8.2 State any one aim of the social reformers who sponsored libraries for millworkers. [1]

    8.3 Evaluate how print helped the poor and the 'low castes' to raise their voice in the twentieth century. Give two examples. [2]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    8.1 Name the book written by Jyotiba Phule about the injustices of the caste system.

    Model answer: Gulamgiri (1871).

    Marking scheme:

    • Gulamgiri (1 mark)

    8.2 State any one aim of the social reformers who sponsored libraries for millworkers.

    Model answer: The reformers wanted to bring literacy to the millworkers. (They also tried to restrict excessive drinking among them and, sometimes, to spread the message of nationalism.)

    Marking scheme:

    • Any one: to bring literacy / to restrict excessive drinking / to spread the message of nationalism (1 mark)

    8.3 Evaluate how print helped the poor and the 'low castes' to raise their voice in the twentieth century. Give two examples.

    Model answer: 1. B.R. Ambedkar in Maharashtra and E.V. Ramaswamy Naicker (Periyar) in Madras wrote powerfully on caste, and their writings were read by people all over India. Local protest movements and sects also created many popular journals and tracts criticising ancient scriptures and imagining a new and just future.
    2. Some workers wrote about their own lives. Kashibaba, a Kanpur millworker, wrote and published Chhote Aur Bade Ka Sawal in 1938 to show the links between caste and class exploitation; the poems of another Kanpur millworker, who wrote as Sudarshan Chakr, were published in a collection called Sacchi Kavitayan.
    Print thus carried the views of the poor and the oppressed to a wide readership.

    Marking scheme:

    • Writings of Ambedkar / Periyar on caste read all over India, or popular journals and tracts of protest movements (1 mark)
    • Workers' writings, e.g. Kashibaba's Chhote Aur Bade Ka Sawal (1938) or Sudarshan Chakr's Sacchi Kavitayan (1 mark)

Section B: Geography

  1. Question 10 · 1 mark · Geography: Resources and Development
    Which of the following correctly describes 'khadar'?

    1. Old alluvial soil with a higher concentration of kanker nodules
    2. New alluvial soil that has more fine particles and is more fertile than bangar
    3. Black soil formed from lava rocks that holds moisture well
    4. Soil formed by intense leaching under heavy rainfall
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) New alluvial soil that has more fine particles and is more fertile than bangar

    According to their age, alluvial soils are classified as old alluvium (bangar) and new alluvium (khadar). Bangar has a higher concentration of kanker nodules; khadar has more fine particles and is more fertile.

  2. Question 11 · 1 mark · Geography: Forest and Wildlife Resources
    Farmers and citizens' groups in India have shown that adequate levels of diversified crop production without the use of synthetic chemicals are possible and economically viable. Which of the following is an example of such a group?

    1. The Green Revolution
    2. The Chipko movement
    3. Beej Bachao Andolan
    4. Joint Forest Management
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) Beej Bachao Andolan

    Farmers and citizens' groups like the Beej Bachao Andolan in Tehri and Navdanya have shown that diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals is possible and economically viable. The Green Revolution was based on package technology (high yielding seeds with fertilisers); in the Chipko movement villagers protected trees by hugging them; Joint Forest Management involves local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests.

  6. Question 15 · 1 mark · Geography: Agriculture
    In the following question, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the appropriate answer from the options given below as the correct answer:
    Assertion (A): Rice is now grown in areas of less rainfall such as Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh.

  7. Question 16 · 2 marks · Geography: Resources and Development
    Every monsoon, rainwater washes away the topsoil from the sloping fields of a hilly district. Suggest two suitable methods of soil conservation for its farmers and explain how each helps.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Contour ploughing: ploughing along the contour lines, instead of up and down the slope, slows down the flow of water down the slope and so reduces the washing away of soil.
    2. Terrace cultivation: steps (terraces) are cut out on the slopes; the flat steps check the speed of running water and restrict erosion. (The Western and Central Himalayas have well-developed terrace farming.)

    Marking scheme:

    • Contour ploughing: ploughing along contour lines slows down the flow of water (1 mark)
    • Terrace farming: steps cut on slopes restrict erosion (1 mark)

  8. Question 17 · 5 marks · Geography: Agriculture
    Agriculture provides livelihood to a large part of India's population. Explain any five technological and institutional reforms introduced by the Government of India to improve Indian agriculture.

    OR 'Though millets are known as coarse grains, they have very high nutritional value.' Describe the conditions of growth and the main producing areas of jowar, bajra and ragi, and give an example of their nutritional value.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Land reform was the main focus of the First Five Year Plan. Collectivisation, consolidation of holdings, cooperation and the abolition of zamindari were given priority after Independence.
    2. In the 1960s and 1970s the government introduced the Green Revolution, based on package technology, and the White Revolution (Operation Flood) to raise production.
    3. Under the comprehensive land development programme of the 1980s and 1990s, crop insurance was provided against drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease.
    4. Grameen banks, cooperative societies and banks were set up to give loans to farmers at lower rates of interest; schemes such as the Kisan Credit Card (KCC) and the Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS) were introduced.
    5. Special weather bulletins and agricultural programmes for farmers were introduced on radio and television, and the government announces minimum support prices, remunerative and procurement prices for important crops to check the exploitation of farmers by speculators and middlemen.

    Marking scheme:

    • Land reforms: consolidation of holdings, cooperation, abolition of zamindari (First Five Year Plan) (1 mark)
    • Green Revolution (package technology) and White Revolution (Operation Flood) (1 mark)
    • Crop insurance against drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease (1 mark)
    • Loans at lower interest from Grameen banks, cooperatives and banks / Kisan Credit Card / PAIS (1 mark)
    • Weather bulletins and farm programmes on radio and TV / minimum support and procurement prices (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: 1. Jowar is the third most important food crop with respect to area and production. It is a rain-fed crop, mostly grown in moist areas, and hardly needs irrigation.
    2. The major jowar-producing states are Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh.
    3. Bajra grows well on sandy soils and shallow black soil. The major bajra-producing states are Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Haryana.
    4. Ragi is a crop of dry regions and grows well on red, black, sandy, loamy and shallow black soils. The major ragi-producing states include Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Jharkhand and Arunachal Pradesh.
    5. Nutritional value: ragi, for example, is very rich in iron, calcium, other micronutrients and roughage.

    Marking scheme:

    • Jowar: rain-fed crop of moist areas, hardly needs irrigation (1 mark)
    • Jowar: Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh (any two) (1 mark)
    • Bajra: sandy and shallow black soils; Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Haryana (any two) (1 mark)
    • Ragi: dry regions; red, black, sandy, loamy soils; e.g. Karnataka, Tamil Nadu (any two states) (1 mark)
    • Nutritional value: ragi rich in iron, calcium, micronutrients and roughage (1 mark)

  9. Question 18 · 4 marks · Geography: Water Resources
    Read the given text and answer the following questions:
    <b>Dams for many uses</b>
    Dams were traditionally built to hold back rivers and rainwater that could be used later to irrigate fields. Today a dam is built not just for irrigation but also for generating electricity, supplying water for homes and industries, controlling floods, recreation, inland navigation and fish breeding. The many uses of the stored water are integrated with one another. The Sardar Sarovar Dam, built over the Narmada River in Gujarat, is one of the largest water resource projects of India and is meant to meet the need for water in drought-prone and desert areas. In recent years, however, such projects and large dams have come under great scrutiny and opposition. Critics point out that the reservoirs created on the floodplains submerge the existing vegetation and soil.

    18.1 Why are such dams called multi-purpose projects? [1]

    18.2 Apart from the problem mentioned in the text, explain two ecological problems caused by multi-purpose projects. [2]

    18.3 Suggest one traditional method that many people see as a viable alternative to large dams. [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    18.1 Why are such dams called multi-purpose projects?

    Model answer: Because the water stored by one dam is used for many purposes at the same time, such as irrigation, electricity generation, water supply, flood control, navigation and fish breeding, and these uses are integrated with one another.

    Marking scheme:

    • The stored water serves several integrated uses (irrigation, power, water supply, flood control, etc.) (1 mark)

    18.2 Apart from the problem mentioned in the text, explain two ecological problems caused by multi-purpose projects.

    Model answer: 1. Damming and regulating rivers affects their natural flow, causing poor sediment flow and excessive sedimentation at the bottom of the reservoir. This results in rockier stream beds and poorer habitats for the rivers' aquatic life.
    2. Dams fragment rivers, making it difficult for aquatic fauna to migrate, especially for spawning.
    (Also accepted: dams built to control floods have triggered floods because of sedimentation in the reservoir; floodplains are deprived of silt, a natural fertiliser; the projects have induced earthquakes, water-borne diseases and pests; a shift to water-intensive crops has caused salinisation of the soil.)

    Marking scheme:

    • Any one problem, e.g. poor sediment flow / excessive sedimentation, rockier stream beds, poorer aquatic habitats (1 mark)
    • Any second problem, e.g. fragmentation of rivers hinders migration of aquatic fauna, floods due to sedimentation, loss of silt on floodplains, earthquakes, water-borne diseases, salinisation (1 mark)

    18.3 Suggest one traditional method that many people see as a viable alternative to large dams.

    Model answer: Rainwater harvesting, for example rooftop rainwater harvesting with underground tankas as in Rajasthan.

    Marking scheme:

    • Rainwater harvesting (e.g. rooftop harvesting, tankas, khadins, johads, guls/kuls) (1 mark)

  1. Question 20 · 1 mark · Civics: Gender, Religion and Caste
    In the following question, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the appropriate answer from the options given below as the correct answer:
    Assertion (A): One-third of the seats in local government bodies, that is, in panchayats and municipalities, are reserved for women in India.
    Reason (R): The Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (Women's Reservation Act, 2023) will give 33 per cent reservation of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and the State Legislative Assemblies.

    1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    3. A is true, but R is false
    4. A is false, but R is true
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A

    Both statements are true. The reservation of one-third of the seats in panchayats and municipalities was made through the Panchayati Raj system; the Act of 2023 concerns the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, so R does not explain A.

  2. Question 21 · 1 mark · Civics: Federalism
    Subjects such as computer software, which came up after the Constitution was made and do not fall in any of the three lists, are called residuary subjects. Who has the power to make laws on them in India?

    1. The State governments
    2. The Union Government
    3. Both the Union and the State governments
    4. The local governments
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) The Union Government

    According to the Constitution, the Union Government has the power to legislate on 'residuary' subjects.

  3. Question 22 · 1 mark · Civics: Gender, Religion and Caste
    A system in which all work inside the home is either done by the women of the family or organised by them through domestic helpers is called:

    1. feminist movement
    2. patriarchy
    3. communalism
    4. sexual division of labour
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) sexual division of labour

    This is the sexual division of labour: boys and girls are brought up to believe that the main responsibility of women is housework and bringing up children, while men do the work outside the home.

  4. Question 23 · 1 mark · Civics: Outcomes of Democracy
    Which of the following is described as the most basic outcome that a democracy should produce?

    1. Rapid economic growth in every year
    2. Complete removal of economic inequality
    3. A government that is accountable to the citizens and responsive to their needs and expectations
    4. Quick decisions without any deliberation
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) A government that is accountable to the citizens and responsive to their needs and expectations

    People have the right to choose their rulers and to control them, so the most basic outcome of democracy should be a government that is accountable to the citizens and responsive to their needs and expectations.

  5. Question 24 · 2 marks · Civics: Power Sharing
    Give one prudential reason and one moral reason in favour of power sharing.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Prudential reason: power sharing reduces the possibility of conflict between social groups. Since social conflict often leads to violence and political instability, power sharing is a good way to ensure the stability of the political order.
    2. Moral reason: power sharing is the very spirit of democracy. People who are affected by its policies have a right to be consulted on how they are to be governed; a legitimate government is one where citizens have a stake in the system.

    Marking scheme:

    • Prudential: reduces conflict between social groups and ensures political stability (1 mark)
    • Moral: spirit of democracy; people have the right to be consulted / legitimate government (1 mark)

  6. Question 25 · 2 marks · Civics: Outcomes of Democracy
    State the two conditions that a democracy must fulfil in order to accommodate social diversity.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Democracy is not simply rule by majority opinion. The majority always needs to work with the minority so that governments function to represent the general view; majority and minority opinions are not permanent.
    2. Rule by majority must not become rule by the majority community in terms of religion, race or linguistic group. Every citizen should have a chance of being in the majority at some point of time.

    Marking scheme:

    • Majority must work with the minority; majority and minority opinions are not permanent (1 mark)
    • Rule by majority must not become rule by a majority community (religion, race, language) (1 mark)

  7. Question 26 · 3 marks · Civics: Gender, Religion and Caste
    The old notions of caste hierarchy are breaking down in modern India. Explain any three factors responsible for this.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Economic development and large-scale urbanisation: people of different castes live and work together in towns and cities, where it is difficult to keep track of caste.
    2. Growth of literacy and education: educated people are more aware of their rights and question caste-based discrimination.
    3. Occupational mobility: children no longer have to follow the occupation of their family or caste, so the link between caste and occupation has weakened.
    (Also: the weakening of the position of landlords in the villages; the efforts of reformers such as Jotiba Phule, Gandhiji, B.R. Ambedkar and Periyar; the Constitution's prohibition of caste-based discrimination.)

    Marking scheme:

    • Economic development and large-scale urbanisation (1 mark)
    • Growth of literacy and education (1 mark)
    • Occupational mobility / weakening of the position of landlords in villages (any third factor) (1 mark)

  8. Question 27 · 5 marks · Civics: Political Parties
    Explain any five functions that political parties perform in a democracy.

    OR Describe the one-party, two-party and multi-party systems with an example of each. Why has India evolved a multi-party system? (3 + 2 = 5)

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Parties contest elections. In most democracies elections are fought mainly among candidates put up by parties; in India, top party leaders choose the candidates.
    2. Parties put forward different policies and programmes, and voters choose from them. A party reduces a vast multitude of opinions into a few basic positions.
    3. Parties play a decisive role in making laws, since most members of the legislature follow the direction of the party leadership.
    4. Parties form and run governments. They recruit leaders, train them and make them ministers.
    5. Parties that lose elections play the role of opposition, voicing different views and criticising the government for its failures or wrong policies.
    (Also: parties shape public opinion by raising and highlighting issues; they provide people access to government machinery and welfare schemes.)

    Marking scheme:

    • Contest elections (1 mark)
    • Put forward policies and programmes for voters to choose from (1 mark)
    • Play a decisive role in making laws (1 mark)
    • Form and run governments (1 mark)
    • Act as opposition / shape public opinion / give people access to government machinery (any fifth function) (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: 1. One-party system: only one party is allowed to control and run the government, for example in China, where only the Communist Party is allowed to rule. It is not a democratic option, because the electoral system does not permit free competition for power.
    2. Two-party system: power usually changes between two main parties; other parties may win a few seats, but only the two main parties have a serious chance of forming the government. Examples: the USA and the United Kingdom.
    3. Multi-party system: several parties compete for power and more than two have a reasonable chance of coming to power, on their own or in alliance with others, for example India. Governments are often formed by a coalition.
    Why India: 4. The social and geographical diversity of such a large country is not easily absorbed by two or even three parties.
    5. A party system evolves over a long time, depending on the nature of society, its social and regional divisions, its history of politics and its system of elections; a multi-party system allows a variety of interests and opinions to enjoy political representation.

    Marking scheme:

    • One-party system with example (China); not a democratic option (1 mark)
    • Two-party system with example (USA / UK) (1 mark)
    • Multi-party system with example (India); coalition or alliance governments (1 mark)
    • India: social and geographical diversity not absorbed by two or three parties (1 mark)
    • Party system evolves with society and history; multi-party system lets many interests be represented (1 mark)

  9. Question 28 · 4 marks · Civics: Federalism
    Read the given text and answer the following questions:
    <b>Two routes to federation</b>
    Federations have been formed in two kinds of ways. In the first, independent States come together on their own to form a bigger unit, so that by pooling sovereignty and retaining their identity they can increase their security. In such federations, all the constituent States usually have equal power and are strong in relation to the federal government. In the second, a large country decides to divide its power between the constituent States and the national government. Here the central government tends to be more powerful than the States, and different constituent units of the federation very often have unequal powers; some units are granted special powers. The exact balance of power between the centre and the states therefore depends mainly on the historical context in which a federation was formed.

    28.1 Name any one country that is an example of a 'coming together' federation. [1]

    28.2 Distinguish between 'coming together' and 'holding together' federations on any two bases. [2]

    28.3 Give one example from India which shows that it is a 'holding together' federation. [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    28.1 Name any one country that is an example of a 'coming together' federation.

    Model answer: The USA (also Switzerland or Australia).

    Marking scheme:

    • USA / Switzerland / Australia (any one) (1 mark)

    28.2 Distinguish between 'coming together' and 'holding together' federations on any two bases.

    Model answer: 1. Formation: a coming together federation is formed when independent States join on their own to form a bigger unit; a holding together federation is formed when a large country divides its power between the constituent States and the national government.
    2. Balance of power: in a coming together federation the constituent States usually have equal power and are strong in relation to the federal government; in a holding together federation the central government tends to be more powerful and the units often have unequal powers.

    Marking scheme:

    • Formation: independent States join together vs. a large country divides its power (1 mark)
    • Power: States equal and strong vs. stronger centre and unequal units (1 mark)

    28.3 Give one example from India which shows that it is a 'holding together' federation.

    Model answer: All States in the Indian Union do not have identical powers: some States, such as Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh and Mizoram, enjoy special powers under certain provisions of the Constitution. (Also accepted: residuary subjects are given to the Union Government.)

    Marking scheme:

    • Some States (e.g. Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram) enjoy special powers, or residuary powers lie with the Union (1 mark)

Section D: Economics

  1. Question 29 · 1 mark · Economics: Development
    The Human Development Report, which compares countries on the basis of the educational levels of the people, their health status and per capita income, is published by:

    1. the World Bank
    2. the Reserve Bank of India
    3. the National Statistical Office
    4. the United Nations Development Programme
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) the United Nations Development Programme

    The Human Development Report is published by the UNDP (United Nations Development Programme). The World Bank, in contrast, uses average or per capita income to classify countries.

  2. Question 30 · 1 mark · Economics: Sectors of the Indian Economy
    Six members of a family work on their small plot of land, but the same output could be produced by only four of them. Even if two members leave, production will not fall. The two extra members are an example of:

    1. disguised unemployment
    2. seasonal employment
    3. regular employment
    4. organised sector employment
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) disguised unemployment

    In disguised unemployment, more people are working than needed; everyone appears to be working, but the contribution of the extra workers to production is nil, so their withdrawal does not reduce output.

  3. Question 31 · 1 mark · Economics: Sectors of the Indian Economy
    A farmer sells wheat to a flour mill for ₹4,000. The mill sells the flour made from it to a bakery for ₹5,500. The bakery uses the flour and other things to make bread, which it sells to consumers for ₹9,000. Which value from this chain is counted in the GDP?

    1. ₹4,000
    2. ₹5,500
    3. ₹18,500
    4. ₹9,000
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) ₹9,000

    Only the value of final goods is counted. Bread is the final good; its value (₹9,000) already includes the value of the wheat and the flour (intermediate goods). Adding all three (₹18,500) would count the same things more than once.

  4. Question 32 · 1 mark · Economics: Money and Credit
    Which institution supervises the functioning of formal sources of loans in India?

    1. The moneylenders' association
    2. The Reserve Bank of India
    3. The World Trade Organisation
    4. The local panchayat
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) The Reserve Bank of India

    The Reserve Bank of India supervises the formal sources of loans: it checks that banks maintain the required cash balance and that they lend to small cultivators, small-scale industries and small borrowers, not just to profit-making businesses. No organisation supervises informal lenders.

  5. Question 33 · 1 mark · Economics: Globalisation and the Indian Economy
    Which of the following best describes globalisation?

    1. Setting up production only within one's own country
    2. Placing restrictions on foreign trade to protect domestic producers
    3. The process of rapid integration or interconnection between countries
    4. Transfer of public sector enterprises to private owners
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) The process of rapid integration or interconnection between countries

    Greater foreign investment and greater foreign trade have led to greater integration of production and markets across countries. Globalisation is this process of rapid integration or interconnection between countries.

  6. Question 34 · 1 mark · Economics: Money and Credit
    In the following question, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the appropriate answer from the options given below as the correct answer:
    Assertion (A): Banks use the major portion of the deposits they receive to extend loans.
    Reason (R): On any particular day, only some of a bank's many depositors come to withdraw cash.

    1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    3. A is true, but R is false
    4. A is false, but R is true
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A

    Since only some depositors withdraw cash on any given day, a bank needs to keep only a small proportion of its deposits as cash and can lend the major portion. R explains A.

  7. Question 35 · 3 marks · Economics: Development
    'Money in your pocket cannot buy all the goods and services that you may need to live well.' Support the statement with three examples.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Normally, money cannot buy a pollution-free environment, unless a person can afford to shift to a community that already has it.
    2. Money cannot ensure that one gets unadulterated medicines.
    3. Money may not be able to protect a person from infectious diseases, unless the whole community takes preventive steps.
    (Also accepted: many things, such as security for a locality or schools for children, are cheaper and better when provided collectively; Kerala has a lower infant mortality rate than Haryana despite a lower per capita income because of adequate provision of basic health and educational facilities.)

    Marking scheme:

    • Pollution-free environment cannot be bought (1 mark)
    • Unadulterated medicines cannot be ensured (1 mark)
    • Protection from infectious diseases needs community action (or another valid example of collective provision) (1 mark)

  8. Question 36 · 3 marks · Economics: Sectors of the Indian Economy
    Explain any three reasons why the government has to spend on a number of activities through the public sector.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Some activities, such as building roads, bridges, railways and harbours, generating electricity and providing irrigation through dams, need large sums of money beyond the capacity of the private sector; collecting money from thousands of users is also not easy, and private firms would charge a high rate. So the government undertakes such heavy spending and makes these facilities available to everyone.
    2. Some activities need government support. For example, selling electricity at the cost of generation may push up the costs of production, and many small-scale units might have to shut down; so the government produces and supplies electricity at rates these industries can afford and bears part of the cost. Similarly, it buys wheat and rice from farmers at a 'fair price' and sells them at a lower price to consumers through ration shops.
    3. Some activities are the primary responsibility of the government, such as providing health and education facilities for all.

    Marking scheme:

    • Activities needing huge spending that the private sector will not provide at a reasonable cost (roads, railways, dams, electricity) (1 mark)
    • Activities that need government support, e.g. cheap electricity for small units or buying grain at a fair price and selling through ration shops (1 mark)
    • Primary responsibility of government: health and education for all (1 mark)

  9. Question 37 · 3 marks · Economics: Globalisation and the Indian Economy
    'Countries can be connected with one another in several ways.' Explain three such ways, and state why one of them has not grown much in recent decades.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Movement of goods and services: through foreign trade, more and more goods and services move between countries, so markets in different countries become integrated.
    2. Movement of investment and technology: investment and technology also move between countries, for example when MNCs invest in production in other countries.
    3. Movement of people: people move from one country to another in search of better income, better jobs or better education. However, in the past few decades there has not been much increase in the movement of people between countries because of various restrictions.

    Marking scheme:

    • Movement of goods and services (foreign trade) (1 mark)
    • Movement of investments and technology (1 mark)
    • Movement of people (for income, jobs, education); not much increase because of restrictions (1 mark)

  10. Question 38 · 5 marks · Economics: Money and Credit
    Study the table given below and answer the questions that follow.
    <b>Sources of loans of households in a town, Navgaon (imaginary data)</b>

    Group of householdsShare of loans from formal sources (%)Share of loans from informal sources (%)
    Poor households3862
    Middle-income households6436
    Rich households8614
    (a) Which group of households depends the most on informal sources of credit? (1)
    (b) Explain two reasons why poor households depend on informal lenders. (2)
    (c) Suggest two ways in which the share of formal credit for poor households can be increased. (2)

    OR Study the table given below and answer the questions that follow.
    <b>Sources of loans of households in a town, Navgaon (imaginary data)</b>

    Group of householdsShare of loans from formal sources (%)Share of loans from informal sources (%)
    Poor households3862
    Middle-income households6436
    Rich households8614
    (a) Which group of households takes the largest share of its loans from formal sources? (1)
    (b) Explain two ways in which high-interest loans from informal lenders harm poor borrowers. (2)
    (c) Why is it important that formal credit is distributed more equally? Give two reasons. (2)

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Poor households: 62 per cent of their loans come from informal sources.
    (b) 1. Banks are not present everywhere, and even where they are, getting a bank loan is much more difficult: banks require proper documents and collateral, and the absence of collateral is one of the major reasons that keeps the poor from bank loans.
    2. Informal lenders such as moneylenders know the borrowers personally and are often willing to give a loan without collateral; borrowers can approach them again even without repaying earlier loans.
    (c) 1. Banks and cooperative societies should increase their lending, particularly in rural areas and to poor households, so that formal credit is distributed more equally.
    2. The poor, particularly women, can be organised into Self Help Groups (SHGs) that pool their savings; after a year or two of regular saving, the group becomes eligible for a bank loan, which is sanctioned in the name of the group without collateral.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Poor households (62 per cent informal) (1 mark)
    • (b) Banks need documents and collateral, which the poor lack / banks not present everywhere (1 mark)
    • (b) Moneylenders know borrowers personally and lend without collateral (1 mark)
    • (c) Banks and cooperatives should lend more to the poor, especially in rural areas (1 mark)
    • (c) Self Help Groups: pooled savings make the group eligible for bank loans without collateral (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: (a) Rich households: 86 per cent of their loans come from formal sources.
    (b) 1. Most informal lenders charge a much higher interest; a larger part of the borrower's earnings goes to repay the loan, so the borrower has less income left.
    2. The amount to be repaid can become greater than the borrower's income, leading to increasing debt and a debt trap. (Also: moneylenders keep no records and may use unfair means to get their money back.)
    (c) 1. Formal loans are cheaper, so the poor could borrow for productive purposes, such as growing crops, doing business or setting up small-scale industries, and so raise their incomes.
    2. At present the rich get cheap formal credit while the poor pay a large amount for borrowing; equal distribution reduces this inequality, and cheap and affordable credit is crucial for the country's development.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Rich households (86 per cent formal) (1 mark)
    • (b) High interest takes a larger part of earnings; less income left (1 mark)
    • (b) Repayment can exceed income, leading to a debt trap (1 mark)
    • (c) Cheaper loans let the poor invest in crops, business or small industry and raise incomes (1 mark)
    • (c) Reduces inequality between rich and poor borrowers; cheap credit is crucial for development (1 mark)
