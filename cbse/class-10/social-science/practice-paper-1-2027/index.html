Section A: History

  1. Question 1 · 1 mark · History: The Rise of Nationalism in Europe
    Which of the following were measures introduced by the French revolutionaries from 1789 to create a sense of collective identity among the French people?
    a. A new French flag, the tricolour, replaced the former royal standard.
    b. Internal customs duties and dues were abolished.
    c. Latin was made the common language of the nation.
    d. Regional dialects were encouraged for use in administration.
    Choose the correct option:

    1. Only a and c
    2. Only a and b
    3. Only b and d
    4. Only c and d
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) Only a and b

    The revolutionaries replaced the royal standard with the tricolour and abolished internal customs duties and dues. French (not Latin) became the common language of the nation, and regional dialects were discouraged, so c and d are wrong.

  2. Question 2 · 1 mark · History: Nationalism in India
    Why did the Khilafat issue offer Gandhiji an important opportunity in 1919-1920?

    1. It made the British agree to restore the powers of the Ottoman emperor.
    2. It led to the immediate repeal of the Rowlatt Act.
    3. It allowed him to bring Hindus and Muslims together in a broad-based movement.
    4. It brought the rich peasant communities of Gujarat into the national movement.
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) It allowed him to bring Hindus and Muslims together in a broad-based movement.

    Gandhiji felt that no broad-based movement could be organised without bringing Hindus and Muslims closer together. The Khilafat issue gave him the opportunity to do this. The British did not restore the Khalifa's powers.

  3. Question 3 · 1 mark · History: The Making of a Global World
    Read the statements given below and choose the correct answer:
    Statement I: Common foods such as potatoes, maize, tomatoes and chillies were not known to our ancestors until about five centuries ago.
    Statement II: These foods were carried to Europe and Asia from China along the silk routes.

    1. Statement I is correct and II is incorrect
    2. Statement I is incorrect and II is correct
    3. Both I and II are correct
    4. Both I and II are incorrect
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) Statement I is correct and II is incorrect

    Statement I is correct. Statement II is incorrect: these foods came from the Americas and reached Europe and Asia only after Christopher Columbus accidentally discovered the Americas, not from China along the silk routes.

  4. Question 4 · 1 mark · History: Print Culture and the Modern World
    Arrange the following events in chronological order, from the earliest to the latest:
    I. Marco Polo brought the knowledge of woodblock printing back to Italy.
    II. Buddhist missionaries from China introduced hand-printing technology into Japan.
    III. Martin Luther wrote his Ninety Five Theses criticising practices of the Roman Catholic Church.
    IV. The Vernacular Press Act was passed in India.
    Choose the correct option:

    1. I, II, III, IV
    2. II, III, I, IV
    3. I, III, II, IV
    4. II, I, III, IV
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) II, I, III, IV

    Hand-printing reached Japan around AD 768-770 (II); Marco Polo returned to Italy in 1295 (I); Luther wrote the Ninety Five Theses in 1517 (III); the Vernacular Press Act was passed in 1878 (IV). So the order is II, I, III, IV.

  5. Question 5 · 2 marks · History: The Making of a Global World
    'The silk routes carried much more than silk.' Support the statement with two examples.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Other goods travelled along the silk routes: Chinese pottery, and textiles and spices from India and South-east Asia, went west, while precious metals such as gold and silver flowed from Europe to Asia in return.
    2. Religion and culture also travelled: Buddhism spread from eastern India in several directions through points on the silk routes, and early Christian missionaries and, later, Muslim preachers travelled along them into Asia.

    Marking scheme:

    • First example, e.g. other goods such as Chinese pottery, Indian and South-east Asian textiles and spices, or gold and silver from Europe, travelled the routes (1 mark)
    • Second example, e.g. spread of Buddhism from eastern India, or travel of Christian missionaries and Muslim preachers along the routes (1 mark)

  6. Question 6 · 3 marks · History: Print Culture and the Modern World
    'Print introduced a new world of debate and discussion in nineteenth-century India.' Explain the statement with reference to religious reform.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Print allowed people to debate religious practices openly. Reformers and the Hindu orthodoxy argued in print over issues such as widow immolation, monotheism, Brahmanical priesthood and idolatry.
    2. Rival views reached a wide public through newspapers: Rammohun Roy published the Sambad Kaumudi from 1821, while the Hindu orthodoxy replied through the Samachar Chandrika; Persian newspapers such as Jam-i-Jahan Nama and Shamsul Akhbar appeared in 1822.
    3. Muslim religious scholars (ulama) used cheap lithographic presses to publish Persian and Urdu translations of holy scriptures and religious newspapers; the Deoband Seminary (founded 1867) published thousands of fatwas telling Muslim readers how to conduct themselves in everyday life.
    4. Religious texts were printed in vernacular languages and reached ordinary people; the first printed edition of Tulsidas' Ramcharitmanas came out from Calcutta in 1810. Tracts in everyday language spread new ideas and shaped the nature of the debate.

    Marking scheme:

    • Point 1 (any one of the points in the model): e.g. open debates in print between reformers and the orthodoxy on widow immolation, idolatry, priesthood (1 mark)
    • Point 2 (a different point): e.g. newspapers such as Sambad Kaumudi (1821) and Samachar Chandrika carried rival views to a wide public (1 mark)
    • Point 3 (a different point): e.g. ulama used lithographic presses for Urdu/Persian translations and fatwas (Deoband, 1867), or vernacular printing of religious texts such as the Ramcharitmanas (1810) (1 mark)

  7. Question 7 · 5 marks · History: The Rise of Nationalism in Europe
    Napoleon introduced several changes in the regions under his control to make the administrative system more efficient. Explain any three of these changes. Why did the local people later turn hostile to French rule? Give two reasons. (3 + 2 = 5)

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Changes (any three):
    1. The Civil Code of 1804 (Napoleonic Code) did away with all privileges based on birth, established equality before the law and secured the right to property.
    2. Administrative divisions were simplified, the feudal system was abolished and peasants were freed from serfdom and manorial dues.
    3. In the towns, guild restrictions were removed, and transport and communication systems were improved.
    4. Uniform laws, standardised weights and measures and a common national currency made the movement and exchange of goods and capital easier for businessmen and small producers.
    Reasons for hostility (any two):
    1. Increased taxation.
    2. Censorship.
    3. Forced conscription into the French armies, which were needed to conquer the rest of Europe. The administrative changes did not go hand in hand with political freedom, so the initial enthusiasm turned to hostility.

    Marking scheme:

    • First change, e.g. the Civil Code of 1804: no privileges based on birth, equality before law, right to property (1 mark)
    • Second change, e.g. simplified administrative divisions, end of the feudal system, peasants freed from serfdom and manorial dues (1 mark)
    • Third change, e.g. guild restrictions removed and transport/communication improved, or uniform laws, weights and measures and a common currency (1 mark)
    • First reason for hostility: increased taxation or censorship (1 mark)
    • Second reason for hostility (different from the first): e.g. forced conscription into the French armies (1 mark)

  8. Question 8 · 4 marks · History: Nationalism in India
    Read the following text and answer the questions that follow:
    <b>The Salt March</b>
    On 31 January 1930, Mahatma Gandhi wrote to the Viceroy, Lord Irwin, listing eleven demands. Some of these concerned industrialists and some concerned peasants, so that every section of Indian society could see its own interest in them. The demand that stirred people the most was the abolition of the salt tax, because salt was eaten by the rich and the poor alike and the government held a monopoly over its production. Gandhiji warned that if the demands were not met by 11 March, the Congress would begin a campaign of civil disobedience. Irwin did not respond. Gandhiji then set out from his ashram at Sabarmati with 78 trusted volunteers and walked for 24 days to the coastal town of Dandi, more than 240 miles away. Large crowds gathered to hear him on the way. On 6 April he reached Dandi and broke the law by making salt from sea water.

    8.1 Why did the demand to abolish the salt tax stir people the most? [1]

    8.2 Why did Gandhiji list demands that concerned different classes, such as industrialists and peasants? [1]

    8.3 Explain how the Civil Disobedience Movement that followed the Salt March was different from the Non-Cooperation Movement. [2]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    8.1 Why did the demand to abolish the salt tax stir people the most?

    Model answer: Salt was consumed by the rich and the poor alike, and the government had a monopoly over its production, so a tax on it affected every Indian and showed the oppressive face of British rule.

    Marking scheme:

    • Salt was used by all, rich and poor, and the government's monopoly/tax on it hurt everyone (1 mark)

    8.2 Why did Gandhiji list demands that concerned different classes, such as industrialists and peasants?

    Model answer: He wanted to make the demands wide-ranging, so that all classes within Indian society could identify with them and be brought together in a united campaign.

    Marking scheme:

    • So that all classes of Indian society could identify with the demands and join a united campaign (1 mark)

    8.3 Explain how the Civil Disobedience Movement that followed the Salt March was different from the Non-Cooperation Movement.

    Model answer: 1. In the Non-Cooperation Movement people were asked only to refuse cooperation with the British (boycott of titles, councils, schools, courts and foreign goods). In the Civil Disobedience Movement people were asked not only to refuse cooperation but also to break colonial laws.
    2. For example, thousands broke the salt law by making salt and demonstrating in front of government salt factories; peasants refused to pay revenue and chaukidari taxes; village officials resigned; and in many places forest people violated forest laws by going into reserved forests to collect wood and graze cattle.

    Marking scheme:

    • NCM asked people only to refuse cooperation (boycott), whereas CDM asked them also to break colonial laws (1 mark)
    • A correct example of law-breaking in CDM, e.g. making salt, refusing to pay revenue/chaukidari tax, violating forest laws (1 mark)

Section B: Geography

  1. Question 10 · 1 mark · Geography: Resources and Development
    Balanced resource planning is needed in India because regions differ in their resources. Read the following descriptions:
    a. Rich in minerals and coal deposits
    b. Very well endowed with solar and wind energy but lacks water resources
    c. Has an abundance of water resources but lacks infrastructural development
    Which state fits description c?

    1. Rajasthan
    2. Jharkhand
    3. Arunachal Pradesh
    4. Chhattisgarh
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) Arunachal Pradesh

    Arunachal Pradesh has an abundance of water resources but lacks infrastructural development. Rajasthan is well endowed with solar and wind energy but lacks water (b); Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh are rich in minerals and coal (a). The example shows why balanced resource planning is needed at national, state, regional and local levels.

    Which of the following options is the correct match?

  4. Question 13 · 1 mark · Geography: Agriculture
    A farmer in Odisha wants to grow a kharif crop in a lowland area with high temperature, high humidity and annual rainfall of more than 100 cm. Which crop is the most suitable?

    1. Wheat
    2. Rice
    3. Mustard
    4. Gram
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) Rice

    Rice is a kharif crop that needs high temperature (above 25 degrees Celsius), high humidity and annual rainfall above 100 cm. Wheat, mustard and gram are rabi crops.

  7. Question 16 · 2 marks · Geography: Water Resources
    Water scarcity can exist even in an area that has plenty of water resources. Explain two reasons for this.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Over-exploitation and excessive use: a large and growing population needs more water for domestic use and to grow more food; rich farmers draw out groundwater through their own wells and tubewells for irrigation, so the water table falls and others get less.
    2. Unequal access among social groups: water may be available, but poorer people may not have access to it.
    3. Poor quality of water: water polluted by domestic and industrial waste, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers used in agriculture becomes unfit for human use.
    4. Industrialisation and urbanisation: industries and large cities use heavy amounts of water and power, and many city housing societies have their own groundwater pumping devices, which over-exploit the resource.

    Marking scheme:

    • First reason explained, e.g. over-exploitation/excessive use by a large population or for irrigation (1 mark)
    • Second reason explained (different), e.g. unequal access, pollution making water unfit for use, or demands of industries and cities (1 mark)

  9. Question 18 · 4 marks · Geography: Minerals and Energy Resources
    Read the given text and answer the following questions:
    <b>Energy for the future</b>
    India's demand for energy is rising fast. Most of it is still met from coal, petroleum and natural gas. These conventional sources will not last for ever, they have to be imported in large quantities in the case of oil, and burning them pollutes the air. This has made it necessary to use energy from sources that can be renewed. India receives bright sunshine for most of the year, and photovoltaic technology turns sunlight directly into electricity. Strong winds along parts of the coast and in the deserts are used to run wind farms; the largest cluster of wind farms stretches from Nagarcoil to Madurai in Tamil Nadu. In villages, shrubs, farm waste and animal and human waste are used to produce biogas. Plants that use cattle dung are known as gobar gas plants.
    (Adapted for this practice paper.)

    18.1 State one reason given in the text for the need to use non-conventional sources of energy. [1]

    18.2 Explain two benefits that gobar gas plants bring to farmers. [2]

    18.3 Why does solar energy have great potential in India? [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    18.1 State one reason given in the text for the need to use non-conventional sources of energy.

    Model answer: Conventional sources such as coal and petroleum will not last for ever (they are exhaustible); also, oil has to be imported in large amounts and burning fossil fuels pollutes the air.

    Marking scheme:

    • Any one: fossil fuels are exhaustible, oil must be imported, or burning them causes pollution (1 mark)

    18.2 Explain two benefits that gobar gas plants bring to farmers.

    Model answer: 1. They give farmers energy for cooking and lighting at low cost; biogas has a higher thermal efficiency than kerosene, dung cake and charcoal.
    2. They also give improved quality of manure, and they prevent the loss of trees and manure that happens when fuel wood and cow dung cakes are burnt.

    Marking scheme:

    • First benefit: cheap energy (fuel) for cooking/lighting, with higher thermal efficiency than kerosene, dung cakes or charcoal (1 mark)
    • Second benefit: improved quality of manure, or saving of trees and manure otherwise burnt as fuel (1 mark)

    18.3 Why does solar energy have great potential in India?

    Model answer: India is a tropical country and receives bright sunshine for most of the year, so it has enormous possibilities of tapping solar energy.

Section C: Political Science

  1. Question 20 · 1 mark · Civics: Federalism
    In the following question, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the appropriate answer from the options given below as the correct answer:
    Assertion (A): In India, the Parliament cannot on its own change the basic arrangement of sharing of powers between the Union and the State governments.
    Reason (R): Any such change has to be passed by both Houses of Parliament with at least a two-thirds majority and then ratified by the legislatures of at least half of the total States.

    1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    3. A is true, but R is false
    4. A is false, but R is true
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A

    Both statements are true. Parliament cannot change the arrangement on its own because the Constitution requires a two-thirds majority in both Houses and ratification by at least half of the State legislatures. R explains A.

  2. Question 21 · 1 mark · Civics: Power Sharing
    Which of the following is an example of power sharing among different social groups?

    1. Separation of powers between the legislature, executive and judiciary
    2. Division of powers between the Union and State governments
    3. The community government in Belgium
    4. A coalition government formed by two or more parties
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) The community government in Belgium

    The community government in Belgium is elected by people belonging to one language community and looks after their cultural, educational and language-related issues. The other options are horizontal sharing, vertical sharing and sharing among political parties.

    a. There is no official religion for the Indian state.

    Why is a democratic government called a legitimate government? Give two reasons.

  7. Question 26 · 3 marks · Civics: Political Parties
    Political parties in India face several challenges. Explain any three of them.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Lack of internal democracy: power is concentrated in one or a few leaders at the top; parties do not keep membership registers or hold regular organisational meetings and elections.
    2. Dynastic succession: top positions are often controlled by members of one family, so ordinary workers rarely rise to the top.
    3. Growing role of money and muscle power: parties tend to nominate candidates who have or can raise lots of money, and some support criminals who can win elections.
    4. Lack of meaningful choice: in many cases the major parties do not offer a meaningful choice because the differences in their policies have reduced.

    Marking scheme:

    • First challenge explained, e.g. lack of internal democracy (1 mark)
    • Second challenge explained, e.g. dynastic succession (1 mark)
    • Third challenge explained, e.g. money and muscle power, or lack of meaningful choice (1 mark)

  8. Question 27 · 5 marks · Civics: Gender, Religion and Caste
    'Women in India still face discrimination and disadvantage in many ways.' Support the statement with five examples.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. Literacy: the literacy rate among women is lower than among men, and a smaller proportion of girls go for higher studies.
    2. Jobs: the proportion of women among highly paid and valued jobs is still very small.
    3. Unpaid work: on average an Indian woman works more hours than a man every day, but much of her work, such as household work, is not paid and often not valued.
    4. Unequal wages: although the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 provides equal wages for equal work, in almost all areas of work, from sports and cinema to factories and fields, women are paid less than men.
    5. Preference for sons: in many parts of India parents prefer sons and find ways to have the girl child aborted, which has led to a decline in the child sex ratio.
    6. Safety: there are many reports of harassment, exploitation and violence against women, both in public places and within the family.
    7. Political representation: the share of women in the Lok Sabha and the State Assemblies has remained low.

    Marking scheme:

    • First example explained, e.g. lower literacy among women (1 mark)
    • Second example explained, e.g. few women in highly paid and valued jobs (1 mark)
    • Third example explained, e.g. unpaid household work, or women paid less than men for the same work despite the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 (1 mark)
    • Fourth example explained, e.g. preference for sons and a declining child sex ratio (1 mark)
    • Fifth example explained, e.g. violence and harassment, or low share of women in legislatures (1 mark)

  9. Question 28 · 4 marks · Civics: Federalism
    Read the given text and answer the following questions:
    <b>Local government in rural India</b>
    The constitutional amendment of 1992 gave new life to local self-government in India. In the countryside this system is called Panchayati Raj. A gram panchayat is set up for each village, though in some States one panchayat serves a group of villages. Its members, the panch, and its head, the sarpanch, are chosen in direct elections by the adults of the ward or village. The gram panchayat works under the overall supervision of the gram sabha, which has to meet at least twice or thrice a year to approve the panchayat's yearly budget and to check how well it has worked. Above the village level, several gram panchayats together form a panchayat samiti, and the panchayat samitis of a district together make up the zilla parishad.

    28.1 Who are the members of the gram sabha? [1]

    28.2 Explain two provisions of the 1992 amendment that made local government more democratic. [2]

    28.3 Who is the political head of the zilla parishad? [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    28.1 Who are the members of the gram sabha?

    Model answer: All the voters in the village are members of the gram sabha.

    Marking scheme:

    • All the voters (adult citizens) of the village (1 mark)

    28.2 Explain two provisions of the 1992 amendment that made local government more democratic.

    Model answer: 1. It is now constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections to local government bodies.
    2. Seats are reserved in the elected bodies and in the executive heads of these institutions for the Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes, and at least one-third of all positions are reserved for women.
    3. An independent State Election Commission has been created in each State to conduct panchayat and municipal elections.
    4. State governments are required to share some powers and revenue with local government bodies.

    Marking scheme:

    • First provision, e.g. regular elections made mandatory, or State Election Commission in each State (1 mark)
    • Second provision, e.g. reservation for SC, ST, OBC and at least one-third for women, or sharing of powers and revenue by State governments (1 mark)

    28.3 Who is the political head of the zilla parishad?

    Model answer: The chairperson of the zilla parishad is its political head.

    Marking scheme:

    • The chairperson of the zilla parishad (1 mark)

Section D: Economics

  1. Question 29 · 1 mark · Economics: Development
    The total income of a country is ₹50,000 crore and its population is 25 crore. What is its per capita income?

    1. ₹200
    2. ₹2,000
    3. ₹20,000
    4. ₹12,500
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) ₹2,000

    Per capita income = total income / total population = ₹50,000 crore / 25 crore = ₹2,000.

  2. Question 30 · 1 mark · Economics: Sectors of the Indian Economy
    Wood cut from a forest is made into tables in a workshop, and the tables are then carried by truck to a shop in the city. To which sectors do these three activities belong, in order?

    1. Secondary, primary, tertiary
    2. Primary, tertiary, secondary
    3. Primary, secondary, tertiary
    4. Tertiary, secondary, primary
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) Primary, secondary, tertiary

    Cutting wood from a forest uses a natural resource (primary); making tables changes the wood into another product (secondary); transport is a service (tertiary).

  3. Question 31 · 1 mark · Economics: Sectors of the Indian Economy
    Which of the following is a feature of the unorganised sector?

    1. Workers get paid leave, holidays and provident fund.
    2. Jobs are regular and working hours are fixed.
    3. Terms of employment are registered with the government.
    4. It is made up of small and scattered units largely outside the control of the government.
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) It is made up of small and scattered units largely outside the control of the government.

    The unorganised sector consists of small and scattered units that are largely outside the control of the government. The other three options describe the organised sector.

  4. Question 32 · 1 mark · Economics: Money and Credit
    A shoe manufacturer wants to exchange shoes directly for wheat, but the wheat grower wants cloth and not shoes. The difficulty faced here is called:

    1. lack of double coincidence of wants
    2. debt trap
    3. lack of collateral
    4. terms of credit
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) lack of double coincidence of wants

    In a barter system, both parties must want what the other is selling. Here the wheat grower does not want shoes, so there is no double coincidence of wants. Money removes this difficulty by acting as a medium of exchange.

  5. Question 33 · 1 mark · Economics: Globalisation and the Indian Economy
    The government of a country puts a tax on toys imported from other countries. This tax is an example of:

    1. foreign investment
    2. liberalisation
    3. a trade barrier
    4. collateral
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) a trade barrier

    A tax on imports is an example of a trade barrier: it is a restriction that the government uses to increase or decrease foreign trade. Liberalisation means removing such barriers.

  6. Question 34 · 1 mark · Economics: Money and Credit
    In the following question, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the appropriate answer from the options given below as the correct answer:
    Assertion (A): Cheap and affordable credit plays an important role in a country's development.
    Reason (R): Banks and cooperatives always give loans without asking for any collateral or documents.

    1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    3. A is true, but R is false
    4. A is false, but R is true
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) A is true, but R is false

    A is true: cheap and affordable credit helps people start businesses, grow crops and increase incomes. R is false: banks usually require proper documents and collateral before giving a loan.

  7. Question 35 · 3 marks · Economics: Development
    In many parts of India, groundwater is being taken out faster than it is replenished by rain. Explain why this is a threat to sustainable development and suggest two ways to deal with it. (1 + 2 = 3)

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Threat: groundwater is a renewable resource only if it is used no faster than it is replenished by rain. Overuse will make the water table fall and the resource will run out, so the present level of development cannot be kept up and future generations will be harmed.
    Ways (any two):
    1. Rainwater harvesting and recharge of groundwater (rooftop harvesting, check dams, recharge pits).
    2. Efficient irrigation such as drip and sprinkler systems.
    3. Growing crops that need less water in water-scarce areas.
    4. Regulating the number of tubewells and the amount of water drawn.

    Marking scheme:

    • Threat: groundwater use beyond replenishment will exhaust it, harming the present level of development and future generations (1 mark)
    • First way, e.g. rainwater harvesting and groundwater recharge (1 mark)
    • Second way (different), e.g. drip/sprinkler irrigation, less water-intensive crops, or regulating tubewells (1 mark)

  8. Question 36 · 3 marks · Economics: Globalisation and the Indian Economy
    Explain how the liberalisation of foreign trade and investment policy by the Indian government around 1991 helped the process of globalisation in India.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 1. After independence, the government had put barriers on foreign trade and investment to protect Indian producers from foreign competition, when industries were just coming up.
    2. Around 1991, the government decided that Indian producers were ready to compete, and it removed barriers on foreign trade and investment to a large extent. Goods could now be imported and exported more easily.
    3. Foreign companies could set up factories and offices in India, and Indian businesses were given more freedom to make their own decisions. This increased the integration of India's markets and production with those of other countries, which is globalisation.

    Marking scheme:

    • Earlier barriers on foreign trade and investment had been used to protect Indian producers (1 mark)
    • From 1991 these barriers were largely removed, so goods could be imported and exported easily (1 mark)
    • Foreign companies could set up factories/offices and businesses gained freedom, integrating India with other countries (1 mark)
