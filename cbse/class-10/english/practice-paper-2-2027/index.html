General instructions

  1. This question paper contains 24 questions. All questions are compulsory.
  2. The question paper is divided into three sections:
    Section A: Reading Skills (20 marks), questions 1 and 2
    Section B: Grammar and Creative Writing Skills (20 marks), questions 3 to 14
    Section C: Literature (40 marks), questions 15 to 24
  3. The board paper has 11 questions. Here they are numbered so that each task can be answered and marked on its own: questions 3 to 12 are the grammar tasks of board question 3; question 13 is the letter (board question 4); question 14 is the analytical paragraph (board question 5); questions 15 and 16 are the extract-based questions (board questions 6 and 7); questions 17 to 20 are from First Flight (board question 8); questions 21 and 22 are from Footprints without Feet (board question 9); questions 23 and 24 are the long answer questions (board questions 10 and 11).

  6. Survey figures, names and places in the reading passages and the writing tasks are imaginary and created for practice.

Section A: Reading Skills

  1. Question 1 · 10 marks · Reading Comprehension
    Read the following passage.

    (1) A generation ago, when a radio stopped working or a shoe lost its sole, the first thought in most Indian homes was, "Who can fix this?" Every neighbourhood had its cobbler, its tailor and its electrician who could bring tired objects back to life. Today the first thought is more often, "Where can I buy a new one?" Repair has quietly given way to replacement, and we are poorer for it in more ways than one.

    (2) Part of the reason is cost. Many electronic goods are now so cheap that a new one costs little more than mending the old one. Some products are deliberately designed so that their parts cannot be opened or replaced. Batteries are glued in, screws are hidden and spare parts are hard to find. Advertisements, too, keep telling us that the latest model is always better. When fixing something becomes difficult, throwing it away begins to feel natural.

    (3) But the price tag in the shop is not the true cost. Every discarded phone, fan or mixer adds to a growing mountain of electronic waste, much of which contains harmful metals that can seep into soil and water. Making a replacement uses fresh raw materials and energy. A repaired object, on the other hand, carries on doing its job without any of these extra demands on the planet.

    (4) There is also a human side to repair. A skilled repair person is a problem-solver who listens to a machine the way a doctor listens to a patient. When repair shops close, these skills disappear, and so do small livelihoods that have supported families for generations. Learning even simple repairs, such as stitching on a button or changing a fuse, also gives young people confidence. They begin to see objects not as mysterious boxes but as things they can understand.

    (5) Around the world, people are pushing back. Volunteer-run "repair cafes" invite people to bring broken items and fix them together over a cup of tea. Some governments have begun to discuss a "right to repair", which would require companies to make spare parts and repair manuals available. Some schools now run workshops where students take apart old gadgets to see how they work.

    (6) None of this means we should never buy anything new. It means pausing before we throw something away and asking whether it deserves a second life. Repair may cost a little time and effort, but the reward is worth it. A world that repairs is a world that wastes less, values skill and respects the things it owns.

  2. Question 2 · 10 marks · Reading Comprehension
    Read the following passage.

    (1) Many Indian towns face water shortages in the summer months, and much of the pressure comes from the way water is used at home. To understand this better, a resident welfare group in Navpur, a mid-sized town, studied how much water an average person in 120 homes used each day and for what purposes.

    (2) The study found that each person used about 135 litres of water a day. The table shows how this was divided.

    ActivityLitres per person per day
    Bathing45
    Flushing toilets35
    Washing clothes25
    Cleaning utensils and floors18
    Drinking and cooking7
    Other uses (gardening, washing vehicles)5


    (3) Only a small share of the water was used for drinking and cooking. Most of it went into bathing, flushing and washing, where much could be saved. The group noted that a bucket bath uses about 20 litres, while a long shower can use 60 litres or more. Old toilet cisterns used up to 12 litres per flush, compared with 6 litres or less for newer dual-flush models.

    (4) The study also found leaking taps in nearly one-third of the homes. A tap that drips all day can waste several litres without anyone noticing. The group advised families to check their taps and pipes regularly.

    (5) The group has now begun a campaign called "Every Drop Counts". It encourages families to fix leaks, use buckets instead of showers and reuse the water from washing vegetables to water their plants.

  3. Question 5 · 1 mark · Articles and Determiners
    Choose the correct option to fill in the blank.
    ________ of my two brothers live in Pune.

    1. Each
    2. Both
    3. Either
    4. Every
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) Both

    'Both' refers to two people together and takes a plural verb ('live'). 'Each' and 'either' would need 'lives'; 'every' cannot be followed by 'of'.

  5. Question 7 · 1 mark · Modals
    Select the correct option to complete the advice.
    You look tired. You ________ take a short break before you continue.

    1. should
    2. ought
    3. used to
    4. has to
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) should

    'Should' gives advice and is followed by the base form. 'Ought' needs 'to', 'used to' describes a past habit, and 'has to' does not agree with 'you'.

  11. Question 13 · 5 marks

Write a letter in about 120 words to the Principal, requesting permission to set up a book bank in the school where students can donate and borrow used textbooks.     Dehradun

    The Principal
    Green Valley School
    Dehradun

  12. Question 14 · 5 marks
    Read the cues below about weekend cycling clubs, which are becoming popular in many Indian cities.
    • improve fitness and mental well-being
    • cost far less than a gym membership
    • use no fuel and cause no pollution
    • build friendships across age groups
    • few dedicated cycle tracks
    • safety risks in heavy traffic
    • extreme heat and heavy rain in some months
    Write an analytical paragraph in about 120 words analysing the reasons for the popularity of cycling clubs and the challenges they face.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Weekend cycling clubs have become popular in Indian cities, and several reasons explain this trend. Firstly, cycling improves physical fitness and also refreshes the mind. Secondly, it costs far less than a gym membership, so many people can afford it. Moreover, since bicycles use no fuel and cause no pollution, many riders see cycling as a way to help the environment. The clubs also build friendships across age groups. However, the trend faces serious challenges. Most cities have few dedicated cycle tracks, so riders must share roads with heavy traffic, which is risky. In addition, extreme heat and heavy rain make cycling difficult in some months. Overall, cycling clubs offer clear benefits, but safer roads and tracks are needed if the trend is to grow.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: the reasons for popularity are analysed using the cues (fitness, low cost, no pollution, friendships) (1 mark)
    • Content: the challenges are analysed (few tracks, traffic risks, weather) and a balanced conclusion is drawn (1 mark)
    • Organisation: an introductory sentence and a concluding statement; no title (1 mark)
    • Organisation: points in a logical order, linked with words such as firstly, moreover, however, overall (1 mark)
    • Accuracy: grammar, spelling and punctuation are correct (1 mark)

  9. Question 23 · 6 marks
    How does the Buddha help Kisa Gotami accept the death of her son, and why is his method more effective than simply telling her the truth? (The Sermon at Benares)

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Kisa Gotami lost her only son. In her grief she carried the dead child to all her neighbours, asking them for medicine, and people said she had lost her senses. The Buddha did not tell her directly that the dead cannot return. Instead, he asked her to bring a handful of mustard seed from a house where no one had lost a child, husband, parent or friend. She went from house to house, but every family had lost someone. Gradually she understood that death is common to all and that her grief had been selfish. Because she discovered this truth for herself, it went deeper than any words could have done. The Buddha's method led her from despair to wisdom and peace.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: her grief: she lost her only son and carried him to her neighbours asking for medicine (1 mark)
    • Content: the Buddha's task: mustard seed from a house where no one had lost a child, husband, parent or friend (1 mark)
    • Content: why it works: she discovers for herself that death is common to all, so the lesson is deeper and lasting (1 mark)
    • Expression: ideas are organised logically, with an opening and a conclusion (1 mark)
    • Expression: the answer is fluent, relevant and in about 100-120 words (1 mark)
    • Accuracy: grammar, spelling and punctuation are correct (1 mark)

  10. Question 24 · 6 marks
    Horace Danby, a careful thief, ends up in prison because of a young lady. Discuss how the title 'A Question of Trust' is ironic. (A Question of Trust)

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Horace Danby was a careful thief who planned the robbery at Shotover Grange in detail. But while he was at work, a young lady surprised him with a voice from the doorway. He trusted her without finding out who she really was, and she played on his fear of being sent to prison. Eager to please her and to be let off, he forgot his own careful methods: he even took off his gloves to give her his lighter. She was in fact a thief herself, and she robbed the safe without leaving a single fingerprint, while Horace ended up in prison. The irony is that a thief, who lives by deceiving others, was ruined because he trusted a stranger blindly. Trust, which a thief can least afford, became his downfall.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: Horace is a careful thief who plans the robbery at Shotover Grange; the young lady surprises him (1 mark)
    • Content: he trusts her blindly; fearing prison, he forgets his caution (takes off his gloves to give her his lighter); she robs the safe without leaving fingerprints and he goes to prison (1 mark)
    • Content: the irony: a deceiver is himself deceived; trusting a stranger becomes his downfall (1 mark)
    • Expression: ideas are organised logically, with an opening and a conclusion (1 mark)
    • Expression: the answer is fluent, relevant and in about 100-120 words (1 mark)
    • Accuracy: grammar, spelling and punctuation are correct (1 mark)
