Section A: Reading Skills

  1. Question 1 · 12 marks
    Read the following passage.

    1. When most people imagine a scientist, they picture someone in a white coat, bent over a microscope in a laboratory. Yet some of the most valuable information about the natural world today is gathered by people with no scientific training at all: schoolchildren, retired teachers, farmers and office workers who spend a weekend morning counting birds or photographing butterflies. This growing movement is known as citizen science.

    2. The idea is not new. For more than a century, volunteers in several countries have counted birds on a fixed day each winter and sent their records to researchers. What has changed is the scale. With smartphones and free apps, a person can now photograph a plant, note the exact time and place, and share the record within seconds. Thousands of such records, pooled together, can reveal patterns that no single research team could ever observe.

    3. In India, too, the movement has taken root. During annual bird counts organised by nature groups, families walk through parks, campuses and village fields, listing the species they see. Their combined lists help scientists track which birds are becoming rarer and which are spreading to new areas. Similar projects record the flowering of trees, the arrival of migratory species and even the quality of water in local lakes.

    4. Scientists value these contributions because nature does not stay in one place. A researcher can study a single forest closely, but changes in climate and land use are happening across an entire country. Only a large network of observers can follow them. Volunteers also notice things that experts might miss, simply because they visit the same patch of land week after week.

    5. The benefits flow both ways. Participants learn to look closely at their surroundings, to tell one species from another and to record what they see with care. Many say that a park they once walked through without a thought has become a place full of discoveries. Teachers report that students who take part in such counts develop patience and a new respect for evidence.

    6. Citizen science is not without its problems. Records sent by beginners may contain mistakes, and some areas, such as remote forests, receive far fewer visitors than city parks. Researchers deal with this by checking unusual records and by using statistical methods that allow for gaps. Yet the movement's greatest gift may be the simplest: it reminds us that science is not only something done for us by experts, but something we can all take part in.

  2. Question 2 · 10 marks
    Read the following passage carefully.

    1. Sonapur, a town of about 1.5 lakh people, has seen a quiet change on its rooftops. In 2022, the town's electricity department surveyed 1,000 households and found that only 60 of them had solar panels on their roofs. When the same number of households was surveyed in 2026, the figure had risen to 210.

    2. Households with rooftop solar reported clear savings. Their average monthly electricity bill fell from about ₹1,800 before the panels were installed to about ₹700 after. Many also said that their homes now had power during short power cuts in the evening, as some systems stored energy in batteries. Officials believe that the falling price of panels, a government subsidy and easy bank loans have encouraged more families to install them. Some families also say that they like producing clean energy at home.

    3. However, most households have still not made the switch. The 790 households without solar panels in 2026 were asked for the main reason. Their answers are given below.

    Main reason for not installing rooftop solarNumber of households
    High initial cost340
    Living in a rented home190
    Not enough roof space, or shade from taller buildings150
    Doubts about maintenance70
    Not aware of the subsidy40
    Total790

    4. The survey also brought out some interesting differences. Families living in their own independent houses were far more likely to have installed panels than those living in apartments, where the roof is shared by many families. Several tenants said that they would like solar power but could not put panels on a building they did not own.

    5. Energy experts suggest a few steps to spread the benefits. Housing societies could install shared systems on common roofs and divide the savings among the residents. Local camps could explain the subsidy and the loan process, and trained technicians could offer low-cost maintenance contracts. With such measures, the experts believe, the town could double the number of solar homes again within a few years.

    Model answer: The peddler, a poor vagabond who sold rattraps, believed that the whole world was a rattrap, with its riches and comforts as the bait. He seemed to prove it himself when he stole thirty kronor from a kind crofter.
    Sophie lives in dreams instead: she will open a boutique, become an actress or a fashion designer. Her mother answers that if she ever had money she should buy them a decent house, and little Derek says she thinks money grows on trees. She even imagines a meeting with Danny Casey and waits on a bench beneath a solitary elm by the canal.
    Their stories end differently. The ironmaster's daughter, Edla, treats the peddler like a captain and lets him stay for Christmas though he may be a thief. Her kindness frees him: he leaves the three ten-kronor notes in a rattrap with a letter signed Captain von Stahle. Sophie's fantasy is never tested against reality, and it leads only to disappointment.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: the peddler's rattrap view (riches as bait) and his theft of the crofter's thirty kronor (1 mark)
    • Content: Sophie's dreams (boutique, actress, fashion designer), her family's response, the imagined meeting with Danny Casey and her wait by the canal (1 mark)
    • Content: the endings: Edla's kindness frees the peddler (notes returned in a rattrap, Captain von Stahle); Sophie's fantasy ends in disappointment (1 mark)
    • Expression: organisation and coherence of ideas (a clear introduction, ideas developed in logical order, a conclusion) (1 mark)
    • Expression: accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation, within about 120-150 words (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: The peasants of Champaran were crushed and fear-stricken; Gandhi told the lawyers that the real relief for them was to be free from fear.
    When the landlords agreed to refund part of the money, Gandhi said that the amount mattered less than the fact that they had been made to surrender part of the money and, with it, part of their prestige. The peasant now saw that he had rights and defenders, and he learned courage.
    Douglas's fear was personal. It began when the surf knocked him down on a California beach at the age of three or four, and it grew when a bruiser of a boy threw him into the deep end of the YMCA pool. He hired an instructor and practised; at Lake Wentworth the terror returned only once, and at Warm Lake he swam across and back without fear.
    The peasants overcame fear together, through a leader; Douglas overcame it alone, through will and practice.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: Indigo: peasants fear-stricken; 'the real relief for them is to be free from fear'; the refund mattered for the planters' lost prestige; the peasant saw he had rights and defenders and learned courage (1 mark)
    • Content: Deep Water: the California beach and the YMCA pool; instructor and practice; Lake Wentworth, Warm Lake; the only thing to fear is fear itself (1 mark)
    • Content: comparison: collective courage through a leader against personal courage through will and persistence (1 mark)
    • Expression: organisation and coherence of ideas (a clear introduction, ideas developed in logical order, a conclusion) (1 mark)
    • Expression: accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation, within about 120-150 words (1 mark)

  12. Question 18 · 5 marks · Vistas: The Third Level
    Attempt any one of the two, (A) or (B), in about 120-150 words.

    A. Derry in 'On the Face of It' and Charley in 'The Third Level' both try to escape from a world that troubles them. Compare their ways of escape and where each of them stands at the end. (On the Face of It; The Third Level)

    OR B. 'The Tiger King' and 'Journey to the End of the Earth' both show the harm that human beings can do to the natural world. Discuss with reference to the two texts. (The Tiger King; Journey to the End of the Earth)

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Derry escapes by keeping away from people. Acid burnt one side of his face - 'It ate my face up. It ate me up' - and he hides from people who stare at him. Mr Lamb, whose gate is always open, treats him with respect and warmth, and Derry decides to return: 'Because if I don't go there, I'll never go anywhere else in this world again.' He chooses to face the world.
    Charley escapes into the past. He finds a third level at Grand Central Station, with gas lamps and brass spittoons, and longs for the Galesburg of 1894. His psychiatrist friend Sam calls it a waking-dream wish fulfilment, born of a world full of insecurity, fear, war and worry. Yet Charley still believes in it, and Sam's letter in a first-day cover suggests that Sam himself has escaped.
    Derry turns towards real life; Charley keeps looking for a way out of it.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: Derry: acid-burnt face, hides from people who stare at him; Mr Lamb's open, accepting attitude; his decision to go back ('I'll never go anywhere else in this world again') (1 mark)
    • Content: Charley: the third level of 1894 (gas lamps, spittoons, old booth); Galesburg; Sam's verdict (waking-dream wish fulfilment; insecurity, fear, war, worry); stamp collecting as a refuge (1 mark)
    • Content: comparison of the endings: Derry faces the world; Charley still seeks escape (Sam's letter in the first-day cover) (1 mark)
    • Expression: organisation and coherence of ideas (a clear introduction, ideas developed in logical order, a conclusion) (1 mark)
    • Expression: accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation, within about 120-150 words (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: In 'The Tiger King', the Maharaja sets out to kill a hundred tigers to defeat a prophecy. He bans tiger hunting by anyone else only so that he can kill them himself. Within ten years he kills seventy tigers, and the tiger population of Pratibandapuram becomes extinct. He then marries a princess from a state with many tigers, and kills five or six tigers on each visit to his father-in-law. Even the hundredth tiger has to be brought from the People's Park in Madras.
    'Journey to the End of the Earth' shows the same harm on a global scale. Antarctica holds half-million-year-old carbon records in its ice cores, and the unmitigated burning of fossil fuels has created a blanket of carbon dioxide around the world, which is slowly increasing the average global temperature.
    One text uses satire about a single ruler's vanity; the other is a warning about all of humanity. Both show that nature pays for human greed and pride.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: The Tiger King: tigers killed for the king's vanity; seventy in ten years, extinct in Pratibandapuram; tigers from the father-in-law's state; the hundredth from the People's Park (1 mark)
    • Content: Journey: carbon records in Antarctic ice cores; fossil fuels and the blanket of carbon dioxide raising global temperature (1 mark)
    • Content: comparison: satire on one ruler against a warning to all humanity; nature pays for human greed and pride (1 mark)
    • Expression: organisation and coherence of ideas (a clear introduction, ideas developed in logical order, a conclusion) (1 mark)
    • Expression: accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation, within about 120-150 words (1 mark)
