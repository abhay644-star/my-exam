Section A: Reading Skills

  1. Question 1 · 12 marks
    Read the following passage.

    1. For thousands of years, millets such as jowar (sorghum), bajra (pearl millet) and ragi (finger millet) were everyday food across much of India. These hardy grasses grow on poor soil with little rain, and they fed farming families through droughts that ruined other crops. Ragi porridge, bajra rotis and jowar bhakris were part of daily meals in many regions. Yet over the last half-century, they slowly disappeared from many Indian plates.

    2. The decline had several causes. From the late 1960s, government policy encouraged farmers to grow high-yielding varieties of rice and wheat, which were bought at assured prices and supplied cheaply through ration shops. Millets received little such support and came to be seen as 'coarse grains', the food of the poor. As incomes rose, families who could afford it switched to polished rice and refined flour, often believing these to be more modern and respectable.

    3. Today, scientists and nutritionists are looking at these grains with fresh eyes. Millets are rich in dietary fibre and minerals; ragi, for instance, is an excellent source of calcium. Because their starch is digested slowly, they release energy gradually, which makes them useful for people trying to control their blood sugar. They are also naturally free of gluten.

    4. Their value to the farmer is just as striking. Many millets need only a fraction of the water that paddy demands, and they can survive high temperatures. Some varieties are ready for harvest in about three months. In a warming world, where rainfall is becoming less predictable, such crops offer a kind of insurance. They also need fewer chemical fertilisers, which lowers costs for small farmers.

    5. The revival is already visible. The United Nations declared 2023 the International Year of Millets at India's suggestion, and millet dosas, biscuits and noodles now appear on supermarket shelves. Several states have added millets to school midday meals and ration supplies. In some districts, farmer groups have set up small units that clean and grind millets locally, so that more of the earnings stay in the village.

    6. Experts warn, however, that a fashionable trend is not the same as a lasting change. Cleaning and processing millets is still laborious, farmers need fair prices for their harvest, and families need simple recipes that suit their daily routines. Only when millets become ordinary food again, and not merely 'superfoods' sold in expensive packets, will this ancient grain truly have returned.

    • Correct option: A. hopeful but cautious, pointing out the challenges that remain (1 mark)

    (ix) Using paragraphs 3 and 4, explain why millets can be described as good for both people and the planet. (Answer in about 40 words)

    Model answer: For people, millets are nutritious: they are rich in fibre and minerals such as calcium, release energy slowly and are gluten-free. For the planet, they need far less water than paddy, survive heat and need fewer chemical fertilisers.

    Marking scheme:

    • People: any two health benefits (fibre, minerals/calcium, slow release of energy, gluten-free) (1 mark)
    • Planet: any two environmental benefits (less water, heat tolerance, fewer chemical fertilisers) (1 mark)

    (x) Find a word from paragraph 6 that means 'needing a lot of hard work'.

    Model answer: laborious

    Marking scheme:

    • Correct word: laborious (1 mark)

Time on social media and videos rose from 95 minutes to two hours, while outdoor sport and play fell from 50 minutes to 35. Reading for pleasure and hobbies also lost a little ground. Time spent with family, however, stayed the same.

  11. Question 17 · 5 marks · Flamingo: Lost Spring
    Answer in about 120-150 words.
    'Lost Spring' shows how poverty and tradition rob children of their childhood. Compare the lives of Saheb and Mukesh, and explain why Mukesh's dream offers some hope.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: In 'Lost Spring', Anees Jung shows how poverty and tradition steal the 'spring' of childhood.
    Saheb's family left Dhaka after storms swept away their fields and homes. In Seemapuri he scrounges for 'gold' in the garbage dumps, because rag-picking is the only way to survive. Later he works at a tea stall, where he is no longer his own master.
    Mukesh belongs to a family of bangle makers in Firozabad. Families there work at glass furnaces in dark, dingy cells, and children often lose their eyesight before they become adults. They are trapped in a vicious circle of sahukars, middlemen, policemen, keepers of the law, bureaucrats and politicians, and accept their plight because of tradition and caste.
    Unlike his elders, Mukesh dares to dream of becoming a motor mechanic and driving a car. His determination shows that even in hopeless conditions, a child's courage to dream can begin to break the cycle.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: Saheb - his family left Dhaka after storms; rag-picking in Seemapuri; at the tea stall he is no longer his own master (1 mark)
    • Content: Mukesh's world - bangle making in Firozabad, furnaces and loss of eyesight, the vicious circle and acceptance of tradition and caste (1 mark)
    • Content: Mukesh's dream of becoming a motor mechanic and his resolve as a sign of hope (1 mark)
    • Expression: organisation and coherence of ideas (a clear introduction, ideas developed in logical order, a conclusion) (1 mark)
    • Expression: accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation, within about 120-150 words (1 mark)

  12. Question 18 · 5 marks · Vistas: The Enemy
    Answer in about 120-150 words.
    Hana is as much the moral centre of 'The Enemy' as Sadao. Discuss.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: In 'The Enemy', Hana shares every risk of Sadao's decision to save the wounded American prisoner of war.
    Unable to throw the helpless man back into the sea, she and Sadao bring him home. When the servant Yumi refuses to have anything to do with him, Hana insists that he is only a wounded, helpless man and herself unties the rugs that cover him. She then helps Sadao to care for the prisoner.
    When the servants leave the house in protest, Hana bears the household work and the fear of discovery. She is so frightened that when a messenger comes from the palace, she thinks he has come to arrest her husband.
    Her compassion comes from her humanity, not from a doctor's duty. Her quiet courage shows that kindness to a helpless human being is greater than hatred of an enemy, which is the central idea of the story.

    Marking scheme:

    • Content: she and Sadao bring the wounded man home; when Yumi refuses to help, Hana herself attends to him and helps care for him (1 mark)
    • Content: she bears the servants' departure and the constant fear of discovery and arrest (1 mark)
    • Content: evaluation - her humanity rises above national hatred, showing the story's central idea (1 mark)
    • Expression: organisation and coherence of ideas (a clear introduction, ideas developed in logical order, a conclusion) (1 mark)
    • Expression: accuracy of grammar, spelling and punctuation, within about 120-150 words (1 mark)
