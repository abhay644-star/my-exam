General instructions

  1. All questions are compulsory.
  2. The question paper has five sections and 33 questions.
  3. Section A has 16 questions of 1 mark each; Section B has 5 questions of 2 marks each; Section C has 7 questions of 3 marks each; Section D has 2 case-based questions of 4 marks each; and Section E has 3 questions of 5 marks each.

Section A: 1 mark each

  1. Question 1 · 1 mark · Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants
    In the seeds of black pepper and beet, a part of the nucellus is not used up and persists in the mature seed. This persistent nucellus is called the:

    1. pericarp
    2. scutellum
    3. perisperm
    4. testa
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) perisperm

    The residual, persistent nucellus in seeds such as black pepper and beet is the perisperm. The pericarp is the wall of the fruit, the scutellum is the cotyledon of a grass embryo and the testa is the outer seed coat.

  2. Question 2 · 1 mark · Human Reproduction
    Which of the following hormones is produced in a woman only during pregnancy?

    1. Luteinising hormone
    2. Human chorionic gonadotropin
    3. Follicle stimulating hormone
    4. Progesterone
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) Human chorionic gonadotropin

    Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) is secreted by the placenta, so it is produced only during pregnancy. LH, FSH and progesterone are also produced during the normal menstrual cycle.

  3. Question 3 · 1 mark · Reproductive Health
    Saheli, an oral contraceptive for women developed at the Central Drug Research Institute (CDRI), Lucknow, is:

    1. a steroidal pill that has to be taken daily
    2. a hormone-releasing device placed in the uterus
    3. a pill that is taken only within 72 hours of unprotected intercourse
    4. a non-steroidal pill that is taken once a week
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) a non-steroidal pill that is taken once a week

    Saheli contains a non-steroidal preparation. It is a once-a-week pill with very few side effects and high contraceptive value.

  4. Question 4 · 1 mark · Principles of Inheritance and Variation
    In honey bees, a female (queen or worker) has 32 chromosomes in each body cell. How many chromosomes are present in a body cell of a male (drone) and in a sperm produced by it?

    1. 16 and 16
    2. 16 and 8
    3. 32 and 16
    4. 32 and 32
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) 16 and 16

    A drone develops from an unfertilised egg by parthenogenesis, so it is haploid with 16 chromosomes. Its sperms are formed by mitosis, so each sperm also has 16 chromosomes.

  5. Question 5 · 1 mark · Principles of Inheritance and Variation
    Human skin colour is controlled by three genes, A, B and C. If two persons with the genotype AaBbCc have children, what fraction of the children is expected to have the genotype aabbcc?

    1. 1/4
    2. 1/8
    3. 1/16
    4. 1/64
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) 1/64

    For each gene, the chance of a recessive homozygote from Aa x Aa is 1/4. For three independently assorting genes: 1/4 x 1/4 x 1/4 = 1/64.

  6. Question 6 · 1 mark · Molecular Basis of Inheritance
    E. coli cells were grown for many generations in a medium containing $\mathrm{^{15}NH_4Cl}$ and were then transferred to a medium containing $\mathrm{^{14}NH_4Cl}$. The cells divide once every 20 minutes. What would the DNA extracted 40 minutes after the transfer consist of?

    1. 100% hybrid DNA
    2. 50% hybrid DNA and 50% light DNA
    3. 25% hybrid DNA and 75% light DNA
    4. 50% heavy DNA and 50% light DNA
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) 50% hybrid DNA and 50% light DNA

    Replication is semiconservative. After one generation (20 minutes) all DNA is hybrid ($\mathrm{^{15}N/^{14}N}$). After the second generation (40 minutes) half the molecules are hybrid and half are light ($\mathrm{^{14}N/^{14}N}$).

  7. Question 7 · 1 mark · Evolution
    Ernst Haeckel proposed that features such as the row of vestigial gill slits seen in all vertebrate embryos support evolution. This proposal was disapproved after careful study by:

    1. Hugo de Vries
    2. Alfred Wallace
    3. Karl Ernst von Baer
    4. Louis Pasteur
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) Karl Ernst von Baer

    Karl Ernst von Baer showed that embryos never pass through the adult stages of other animals, and so disapproved Haeckel's embryological proposal.

  8. Question 8 · 1 mark · Human Health and Disease
    During an allergic reaction to pollen grains, mast cells release chemicals such as histamine and serotonin. Which class of antibodies is produced in this response?

    1. IgA
    2. IgG
    3. IgM
    4. IgE
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) IgE

    Allergy is an exaggerated response of the immune system to allergens. The antibodies produced are of the IgE type, and mast cells then release histamine and serotonin.

  9. Question 9 · 1 mark · Microbes in Human Welfare
    Statins, used to lower blood cholesterol, are produced by the yeast Monascus purpureus. Statins act by:

    1. competitively inhibiting the enzyme responsible for the synthesis of cholesterol
    2. dissolving the cholesterol deposited in blood vessels
    3. killing the bacteria that produce cholesterol in the gut
    4. suppressing the immune response of the body
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) competitively inhibiting the enzyme responsible for the synthesis of cholesterol

    Statins resemble the substrate of the enzyme that makes cholesterol, and competitively inhibit it, so less cholesterol is made.

  10. Question 10 · 1 mark · Biotechnology: Principles and Processes
    Which of the following DNA sequences is a palindrome, that is, it reads the same on the two strands when both are read in the 5' $\rightarrow$ 3' direction?

    1. 5'-GGATCG-3'
    2. 5'-GAATTC-3'
    3. 5'-AAGCTA-3'
    4. 5'-CTGCAT-3'
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) 5'-GAATTC-3'

    The complementary strand of 5'-GAATTC-3' is 3'-CTTAAG-5', which read 5' to 3' is again GAATTC. For each of the others, the complementary strand read 5' to 3' is different (CGATCC, TAGCTT and ATGCAG).

  11. Question 11 · 1 mark · Biotechnology and its Applications
    Which cry gene of Bacillus thuringiensis codes for a toxin that protects corn plants from the corn borer?

    1. cryIAc
    2. cryIIAb
    3. cryIAb
    4. lacZ
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) cryIAb

    The proteins coded by cryIAc and cryIIAb control cotton bollworms, while the protein coded by cryIAb controls the corn borer. lacZ is not a cry gene.

  12. Question 12 · 1 mark · Human Health and Disease
    Cannabinoids, obtained from the inflorescences of Cannabis sativa, are known mainly for their effects on the:

    1. cardiovascular system
    2. liver
    3. kidneys
    4. skeletal muscles
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) cardiovascular system

    Cannabinoids interact with cannabinoid receptors present mainly in the brain and are known for their effects on the cardiovascular system of the body.

  13. Question 13 · 1 mark · Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants
    Assertion (A): Pollen grains are well preserved as fossils.
    Reason (R): The exine of a pollen grain is made of cellulose and pectin.

    1. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
    2. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
    3. A is true but R is false.
    4. A is false but R is true.
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) A is true but R is false.

    A is true: pollen grains are well preserved as fossils because the exine contains sporopollenin, which withstands high temperatures, strong acids and alkalis and is not degraded by any known enzyme. R is false: cellulose and pectin make up the intine.

  14. Question 14 · 1 mark · Molecular Basis of Inheritance
    Assertion (A): The human genome contains about $3 \times 10^9$ base pairs.
    Reason (R): Less than 2 per cent of the human genome codes for proteins.

    1. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
    2. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
    3. A is true but R is false.
    4. A is false but R is true.
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.

    Both statements are findings of the Human Genome Project, and both are true. However, the small proportion of protein-coding DNA does not explain the total size of the genome, so R is not the correct explanation of A.

  15. Question 15 · 1 mark · Ecosystem
    Assertion (A): Primary productivity is the same in all ecosystems.
    Reason (R): Primary productivity depends on the plant species of an area, environmental factors, availability of nutrients and the photosynthetic capacity of plants.

    1. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
    2. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
    3. A is true but R is false.
    4. A is false but R is true.
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) A is false but R is true.

    A is false: because primary productivity depends on all the factors given in R, it varies widely between ecosystems. R is true.

  16. Question 16 · 1 mark · Biodiversity and Conservation
    Assertion (A): Tropical regions have greater species diversity than temperate regions.
    Reason (R): Tropical latitudes have remained relatively undisturbed for millions of years and so have had a long evolutionary time for species diversification.

    1. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
    2. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
    3. A is true but R is false.
    4. A is false but R is true.
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.

    Both are true. Temperate regions went through frequent glaciations in the past, while the tropics had a long, undisturbed evolutionary time; this is one accepted explanation of the greater diversity in the tropics.

Section B: 2 marks each

  1. Question 17 · 2 marks · Human Reproduction
    A woman has reached the end of her pregnancy.
    (a) What signals start the process of parturition?
    (b) Explain the role of oxytocin in parturition.

    OR The placenta is the structural and functional unit between the developing embryo and the mother's body.
    (a) State two roles of the placenta in the exchange of materials.
    (b) Name two hormones secreted by the placenta.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Signals for parturition come from the fully developed foetus and the placenta. They induce mild contractions of the uterus, called the foetal ejection reflex.
    (b) The foetal ejection reflex triggers the release of oxytocin from the maternal pituitary. Oxytocin acts on the uterine muscle and causes stronger contractions, which in turn stimulate further secretion of oxytocin. These stronger and stronger contractions lead to the expulsion of the baby through the birth canal.

    Marking scheme:

    • Signals from the fully developed foetus and the placenta (½ marks)
    • Mild uterine contractions: foetal ejection reflex (½ marks)
    • Oxytocin is released from the maternal pituitary (½ marks)
    • Oxytocin causes stronger uterine contractions that expel the baby (½ marks)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: (a) The placenta supplies oxygen and nutrients from the mother's blood to the embryo, and removes carbon dioxide and excretory (waste) materials produced by the embryo.
    (b) Any two: human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), human placental lactogen (hPL), oestrogens, progestogens.

    Marking scheme:

    • Supplies oxygen and nutrients to the embryo (½ marks)
    • Removes carbon dioxide and waste materials from the embryo (½ marks)
    • First correct hormone (½ marks)
    • Second correct hormone (½ marks)

  2. Question 18 · 2 marks · Principles of Inheritance and Variation
    A child has a small round head, a furrowed tongue, a partially open mouth and a broad palm with a characteristic palm crease. The child's physical, psychomotor and mental development is retarded.
    (a) Name the disorder and state its chromosomal cause.
    (b) How does such a chromosomal change arise?

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Down's syndrome. It is caused by the presence of an additional copy of chromosome 21 (trisomy of chromosome 21).
    (b) It arises from the failure of segregation of chromatids (or homologous chromosomes) during cell division, usually during gamete formation. A gamete with an extra chromosome 21 then fuses with a normal gamete.

    Marking scheme:

    • Down's syndrome (½ marks)
    • Trisomy of chromosome 21 (½ marks)
    • Failure of segregation of chromatids/chromosomes during cell division (gamete formation) (½ marks)
    • A gamete carrying an extra chromosome 21 takes part in fertilisation (½ marks)

  3. Question 19 · 2 marks · Microbes in Human Welfare
    A farmer grows pulses in one field and paddy in another. He wants to reduce the use of chemical fertilisers. Suggest one biofertiliser for each field and explain how it helps.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Pulses: Rhizobium. These bacteria live in the root nodules of leguminous plants and fix atmospheric nitrogen into organic forms that the plant can use.
    Paddy: cyanobacteria such as Anabaena, Nostoc or Oscillatoria. They are free-living (autotrophic) organisms that fix atmospheric nitrogen in paddy fields and also add organic matter to the soil, increasing its fertility.

    Marking scheme:

    • Pulses: Rhizobium (½ marks)
    • Fixes atmospheric nitrogen in root nodules of the legume (½ marks)
    • Paddy: cyanobacteria (Anabaena / Nostoc / Oscillatoria) (½ marks)
    • Fix atmospheric nitrogen and add organic matter to the soil (½ marks)

  4. Question 20 · 2 marks · Biotechnology and its Applications
    Genetically modified (GM) plants have been useful to farmers in many ways. State four of them.

    OR Explain how tobacco plants were made resistant to the nematode Meloidogyne incognita by RNA interference (RNAi).

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Any four: GM plants (1) are more tolerant to abiotic stresses such as cold, drought, salt and heat; (2) have reduced the reliance on chemical pesticides (pest-resistant crops); (3) have helped to reduce post-harvest losses; (4) use minerals more efficiently, which prevents early exhaustion of the fertility of soil; (5) have enhanced nutritional value of food.

    Marking scheme:

    • First correct use (½ marks)
    • Second correct use (½ marks)
    • Third correct use (½ marks)
    • Fourth correct use (½ marks)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: The nematode Meloidogyne incognita infects the roots of tobacco plants. Nematode-specific genes were introduced into the host plant using Agrobacterium vectors. The genes were made to produce both sense and anti-sense RNA in the host cells. These two RNAs, being complementary, formed double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), which started RNAi and silenced the specific mRNA of the nematode. As a result, the parasite could not survive in the transgenic host, and the plant was protected.

    Marking scheme:

    • Nematode-specific genes introduced into tobacco using Agrobacterium vectors (½ marks)
    • Host cells produce both sense and anti-sense RNA (½ marks)
    • The complementary RNAs form dsRNA, which starts RNAi (½ marks)
    • Specific mRNA of the nematode is silenced; the parasite cannot survive (½ marks)

  5. Question 21 · 2 marks · Molecular Basis of Inheritance
    (a) The genetic code is said to be degenerate. What does this mean? Give an example.
    (b) What is meant by saying that the genetic code is nearly universal? Give an example.

    OR Describe the experiment of Frederick Griffith with Streptococcus pneumoniae and mice, and state the conclusion he drew.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Some amino acids are coded by more than one codon. For example, both UUU and UUC code for phenylalanine.
    (b) The same codon codes for the same amino acid in almost all organisms, from bacteria to human beings (a few exceptions are known, for example in mitochondria). For example, UUU codes for phenylalanine in bacteria as well as in humans.

    Marking scheme:

    • Degenerate: an amino acid can be coded by more than one codon (½ marks)
    • Correct example (e.g. UUU and UUC for phenylalanine) (½ marks)
    • Universal: a codon codes for the same amino acid in nearly all organisms (½ marks)
    • Correct example (e.g. UUU codes for phenylalanine from bacteria to humans) (½ marks)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: Griffith used two strains: the S strain (smooth, with a polysaccharide capsule) killed mice, while the R strain (rough, no capsule) did not. Heat-killed S strain bacteria did not kill mice. But when heat-killed S strain bacteria were injected together with live R strain bacteria, the mice died, and live S strain bacteria were recovered from the dead mice. He concluded that the R strain bacteria had been transformed by some 'transforming principle' passed on from the heat-killed S strain, which enabled them to make a smooth capsule and become virulent.

    Marking scheme:

    • S strain kills mice; R strain does not (½ marks)
    • Heat-killed S strain alone does not kill mice (½ marks)
    • Heat-killed S + live R kills mice; live S bacteria recovered from them (½ marks)
    • Conclusion: R strain transformed by a 'transforming principle' from the heat-killed S strain (½ marks)

Section C: 3 marks each

  1. Question 22 · 3 marks · Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants
    In most flowering plants, the female gametophyte (embryo sac) develops from a single functional megaspore.
    (a) What is this type of development called? What happens to the other three megaspores?
    (b) How many mitotic divisions does the nucleus of the functional megaspore undergo to form the mature embryo sac? Why are these divisions called free nuclear divisions?
    (c) Describe the arrangement of cells in the mature embryo sac and explain why it is called 7-celled and 8-nucleate.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Monosporic development. The other three megaspores degenerate.
    (b) Three successive mitotic divisions (giving the 2-, 4- and 8-nucleate stages). They are called free nuclear because the nuclear divisions are not followed immediately by cell wall formation.
    (c) At the micropylar end there are three cells forming the egg apparatus (two synergids and one egg cell). At the chalazal end there are three antipodal cells. The large central cell has two polar nuclei. So there are 7 cells (6 cells with one nucleus each and the central cell) but 8 nuclei.

    Marking scheme:

    • Monosporic development (½ marks)
    • Other three megaspores degenerate (½ marks)
    • Three mitotic divisions (½ marks)
    • Nuclear divisions are not followed immediately by cell wall formation (½ marks)
    • Egg apparatus (egg + two synergids) at the micropylar end, three antipodals at the chalazal end, central cell with two polar nuclei (½ marks)
    • Six uninucleate cells + binucleate central cell = 7 cells, 8 nuclei (½ marks)

  3. Question 24 · 3 marks · Molecular Basis of Inheritance
    The nucleus of a diploid cell of a certain plant contains a total of $5 \times 10^9$ base pairs of DNA. The distance between two consecutive base pairs is 0.34 nm ($0.34 \times 10^{-9}$ m).
    (a) Calculate the total length of DNA in this nucleus.
    (b) Explain how such a long DNA molecule is packed inside the nucleus.
    (c) Distinguish between euchromatin and heterochromatin.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Length $= 5 \times 10^9 \times 0.34 \times 10^{-9}$ m $= 1.7$ m.
    (b) The negatively charged DNA is wrapped around a positively charged histone octamer to form a nucleosome; a typical nucleosome contains about 200 bp of DNA. Nucleosomes repeat to form a 'beads-on-string' structure, which is coiled into chromatin fibres; these are further coiled and condensed (with the help of non-histone chromosomal proteins) to form chromosomes.
    (c) Euchromatin is loosely packed, stains light and is transcriptionally active. Heterochromatin is densely packed, stains dark and is transcriptionally inactive.

    Marking scheme:

    • Correct working: $5 \times 10^9 \times 0.34 \times 10^{-9}$ m (½ marks)
    • Length = 1.7 m (½ marks)
    • Negatively charged DNA wrapped around a positively charged histone octamer (nucleosome) (½ marks)
    • Nucleosomes (beads-on-string) coiled into chromatin fibres and further condensed (½ marks)
    • Euchromatin: loosely packed, light-staining, transcriptionally active (½ marks)
    • Heterochromatin: densely packed, dark-staining, inactive (½ marks)

  4. Question 25 · 3 marks · Principles of Inheritance and Variation
    (a) What change in the haemoglobin molecule causes sickle-cell anaemia, and what change in the gene is responsible for it?
    (b) A man and a woman, both carriers of sickle-cell anaemia ($\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^S}$), plan to have a child. What is the probability that the child will suffer from the disease? Show the cross.
    (c) How does the defect in thalassemia differ from that in sickle-cell anaemia?

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Glutamic acid (Glu) is replaced by valine (Val) at the sixth position of the beta globin chain of haemoglobin. This is due to a single base substitution at the sixth codon of the beta globin gene, from GAG to GUG.
    (b) Parents: $\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^S}$ x $\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^S}$. Gametes from each parent: $\mathrm{Hb^A}$ and $\mathrm{Hb^S}$.
    Offspring: $\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^A}$ (normal) : $\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^S}$ (carrier) : $\mathrm{Hb^S Hb^S}$ (diseased) = 1 : 2 : 1.
    Probability of a diseased child = 1/4 (25 per cent).
    (c) Thalassemia is a quantitative problem: too few globin molecules (of one of the chains) are synthesised. Sickle-cell anaemia is a qualitative problem: an incorrectly functioning globin is synthesised.

    Marking scheme:

    • Glu replaced by Val at the sixth position of the beta globin chain (½ marks)
    • Single base substitution, GAG to GUG (½ marks)
    • Correct cross giving $\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^A}$ : $\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^S}$ : $\mathrm{Hb^S Hb^S}$ = 1 : 2 : 1 (½ marks)
    • Probability = 1/4 (½ marks)
    • Thalassemia: quantitative, too few globin chains made (½ marks)
    • Sickle-cell: qualitative, incorrectly functioning globin made (½ marks)

  6. Question 27 · 3 marks · Biotechnology and its Applications
    (a) What are transgenic animals?
    (b) Explain how transgenic animals are used in (i) testing the safety of vaccines and (ii) producing biological products. Give one example of each.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Transgenic animals are animals whose DNA has been manipulated to possess and express an extra (foreign) gene.

    (ii) Transgenic animals carrying the gene for a useful protein produce that protein, for example in their milk. For example, human protein alpha-1-antitrypsin, used to treat emphysema, has been produced by transgenic animals.

    Marking scheme:

    • Animals whose DNA is manipulated to possess and express an extra (foreign) gene (1 mark)
    • Used to test the safety of vaccines before use on humans (½ marks)
    • Example: transgenic mice for the polio vaccine (½ marks)
    • Animals made to produce useful proteins (biological products) (½ marks)
    • Example: alpha-1-antitrypsin for emphysema (½ marks)

  7. Question 28 · 3 marks · Ecosystem
    (a) Name the two main types of food chains in an ecosystem and state what each begins with.
    (b) Through which type of food chain does the larger fraction of energy flow in (i) an aquatic ecosystem and (ii) a terrestrial ecosystem?
    (c) What are detritivores? Give one example.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) The grazing food chain (GFC), which begins with the producers (green plants), and the detritus food chain (DFC), which begins with dead organic matter (detritus).
    (b) (i) In an aquatic ecosystem, the grazing food chain is the major conduit for energy flow. (ii) In a terrestrial ecosystem, a much larger fraction of energy flows through the detritus food chain.
    (c) Detritivores are organisms that feed on detritus and break it down into smaller particles (fragmentation). Example: earthworm.

    Marking scheme:

    • Grazing food chain begins with producers (½ marks)
    • Detritus food chain begins with dead organic matter (½ marks)
    • Aquatic: grazing food chain (½ marks)
    • Terrestrial: detritus food chain (½ marks)
    • Detritivores feed on detritus and break it into smaller particles (½ marks)
    • Example: earthworm (½ marks)

Section D: case-based, 4 marks each

  1. Question 29 · 4 marks · Human Reproduction
    Arun, aged 28, and his wife have been trying to have a child for three years. Semen analysis shows that Arun has a very low sperm count. Blood tests show that the levels of luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) in his blood are low. The doctor explains that his pituitary is not receiving enough of the signal that normally comes from the hypothalamus.

    (i) Name the hypothalamic hormone whose low secretion could explain the low levels of LH and FSH. [1]

    (ii) Explain how LH and FSH normally act on the testes to support spermatogenesis. [2]

    (iii) Name the cells in the seminiferous tubules that provide nutrition to the developing germ cells. [1]

    OR (iii) A primary spermatocyte completes the first meiotic division. Name the cells formed and state their ploidy.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    (i) Name the hypothalamic hormone whose low secretion could explain the low levels of LH and FSH.

    Model answer: Gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH).

    Marking scheme:

    • GnRH (gonadotropin releasing hormone) (1 mark)

    (ii) Explain how LH and FSH normally act on the testes to support spermatogenesis.

    Model answer: LH acts on the Leydig (interstitial) cells and stimulates the synthesis and secretion of androgens, which in turn stimulate the process of spermatogenesis. FSH acts on the Sertoli cells and stimulates the secretion of some factors which help in the process of spermiogenesis.

    Marking scheme:

    • LH acts on Leydig cells (½ marks)
    • Androgens secreted, which stimulate spermatogenesis (½ marks)

    (iii) Name the cells in the seminiferous tubules that provide nutrition to the developing germ cells.

  1. Question 31 · 5 marks · Evolution
    (i) Name the two key concepts of Darwin's theory of evolution.
    (ii) What did Darwin mean by 'fitness'?
    (iii) How did Hugo de Vries's idea of how new species arise differ from Darwin's?
    (iv) A trait in a population shows a bell-shaped (normal) distribution. Describe the three ways in which natural selection can act on it, stating what happens to the distribution in each case. Which of them can split the population into two forms?

    OR (i) About 15 million years ago, the primates Dryopithecus and Ramapithecus existed. Which of them was more man-like?
    (ii) State the brain capacity and the food habit of Homo habilis.
    (iii) State the brain capacity of Neanderthal man and two features of their way of life.
    (iv) Where did Homo sapiens arise? About how long ago did agriculture begin?
    (v) Where did the Australopithecines live, and what did they mainly eat?

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (i) Branching descent and natural selection.
    (ii) Fitness, according to Darwin, refers ultimately and only to reproductive fitness: those who are better fit in an environment leave more progeny than others.
    (iii) De Vries, working on evening primrose, proposed that it is mutation (large, sudden differences arising in a population) that causes evolution, so a new species can arise in a single step (saltation). Darwin believed that evolution takes place gradually through small variations that are directional.
    (iv) Stabilising selection: more individuals acquire the mean character value; the peak becomes higher and narrower.
    Directional selection: more individuals acquire a value other than the mean (towards one end); the peak shifts in one direction.
    Disruptive selection: more individuals acquire the peripheral values at both ends of the distribution; two peaks are formed. Disruptive selection can split the population into two forms.

    Marking scheme:

    • Branching descent and natural selection (1 mark)
    • Fitness = reproductive fitness (leaving more progeny) (1 mark)
    • De Vries: large sudden mutations cause evolution in a single step (saltation); Darwin: small, gradual, directional variations (1 mark)
    • Stabilising: more individuals with the mean value (½ marks)
    • Directional: more individuals with a value other than the mean (one end) (½ marks)
    • Disruptive: more individuals at both ends of the curve (½ marks)
    • Disruptive selection can split the population into two forms (½ marks)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: (i) Ramapithecus was more man-like; Dryopithecus was more ape-like.
    (ii) Homo habilis had a brain capacity of 650-800 cc. They probably did not eat meat.
    (iii) Neanderthal man had a brain size of 1400 cc. They used hides to protect their bodies and buried their dead. (They lived in the near east and central Asian countries between 1,00,000 and 40,000 years ago.)
    (iv) Homo sapiens arose in Africa and moved across continents. Agriculture began about 10,000 years ago.
    (v) The Australopithecines probably lived in the East African grasslands about 2 million years ago. They hunted with stone weapons but essentially ate fruit.

    Marking scheme:

    • Ramapithecus (1 mark)
    • Homo habilis: 650-800 cc (½ marks)
    • Probably did not eat meat (½ marks)
    • Neanderthal: 1400 cc (½ marks)
    • Used hides to protect the body and buried their dead (½ marks)
    • Homo sapiens arose in Africa (½ marks)
    • Agriculture about 10,000 years ago (½ marks)
    • East African grasslands (½ marks)
    • Hunted with stone weapons but mainly ate fruit (½ marks)

  2. Question 32 · 5 marks · Biotechnology: Principles and Processes
    A scientist wants to clone a gene of interest in E. coli.
    (i) The DNA is cut with the restriction enzyme EcoRI. What are sticky ends, and why are they useful?
    (ii) How are the DNA fragments separated, seen and recovered by agarose gel electrophoresis?
    (iii) The gene is inserted into the lacZ gene of a vector. Explain how colonies with recombinant plasmids can be told apart from colonies with non-recombinant plasmids.

    OR (i) Why are bioreactors needed for producing biotechnology products?
    (ii) Describe the main features of a stirred-tank bioreactor.
    (iii) What is downstream processing? Why must the product pass through strict quality control and, in the case of drugs, clinical trials?

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (i) Restriction enzymes cut the two strands of DNA a little away from the centre of the palindrome, between the same two bases on each strand. This leaves single-stranded overhanging stretches at the ends, called sticky ends. They form hydrogen bonds with their complementary cut counterparts, which helps DNA ligase to join DNA fragments (for example, the gene and the vector cut with the same enzyme).
    (ii) DNA is negatively charged, so in an electric field the fragments move towards the anode through the agarose gel, which acts as a sieve; the smaller the fragment, the farther it moves, so the fragments are separated according to size. The DNA is stained with ethidium bromide and seen as bright orange bands under ultraviolet light. The bands are cut out from the gel and the DNA is extracted from the gel piece (elution).
    (iii) The lacZ gene codes for the enzyme beta-galactosidase. When the gene of interest is inserted into lacZ, the enzyme is not made (insertional inactivation). The bacteria are grown on a medium containing a chromogenic substrate: colonies with non-recombinant plasmids make the enzyme and turn blue, while colonies with recombinant plasmids do not make the enzyme and stay colourless (white).

    Marking scheme:

    • Sticky ends: single-stranded overhangs left when the enzyme cuts away from the centre of the palindrome (½ marks)
    • They pair (hydrogen bond) with complementary ends, helping DNA ligase join fragments (½ marks)
    • Negatively charged DNA moves towards the anode through the gel (½ marks)
    • Fragments separate by size: smaller ones move farther (½ marks)
    • Stained with ethidium bromide; orange bands seen under UV light (½ marks)
    • Bands cut out and DNA extracted (elution) (½ marks)
    • lacZ codes for beta-galactosidase; insertion inactivates it (insertional inactivation) (1 mark)
    • On a chromogenic substrate, non-recombinants give blue colonies and recombinants give colourless colonies (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: (i) To produce a product in large quantities, cells have to be grown in large volumes of culture (100 to 1000 litres). Bioreactors are large vessels in which such volumes can be grown under optimal conditions (temperature, pH, substrate, salts, vitamins, oxygen).
    (ii) A stirred-tank bioreactor is usually cylindrical or has a curved base to help in mixing the contents. The stirrer mixes the contents evenly and makes oxygen available throughout the bioreactor (alternatively, sterile air is bubbled through it). It has an agitator system, an oxygen delivery system and a foam control system, together with a temperature control system, a pH control system and sampling ports from which small volumes of the culture can be withdrawn.
    (iii) Downstream processing is the set of processes the product goes through after biosynthesis before it is ready for marketing: separation and purification, and formulation with suitable preservatives. Drugs must go through thorough clinical trials, and every product needs strict quality control testing, to make sure that the product is safe and effective.

    Marking scheme:

    • Large volumes of culture are needed for large-scale production (1 mark)
    • Cylindrical vessel / curved base; stirrer mixes contents and makes oxygen available throughout (1 mark)
    • Agitator, oxygen delivery and foam control systems (½ marks)
    • Temperature control, pH control and sampling ports (½ marks)
    • Separation and purification of the product (½ marks)
    • Formulation with suitable preservatives (½ marks)
    • Clinical trials for drugs (½ marks)
    • Strict quality control testing to ensure safety and effectiveness (½ marks)

  3. Question 33 · 5 marks · Organisms and Populations
    A population of field mice in a grassland has an intrinsic rate of natural increase r = 0.2 per year. The carrying capacity of the grassland for these mice is K = 1000.
    (i) Write the equation for exponential growth of a population and name the shape of the growth curve.
    (ii) Write the equation for logistic growth and name the shape of its growth curve. What does K represent?
    (iii) Using the logistic model, calculate the rate of growth of the mouse population ($dN/dt$) when N = 200.
    (iv) Why is the logistic growth model considered more realistic?

    OR (i) State Gause's competitive exclusion principle.
    (ii) MacArthur found five closely related species of warblers living on the same tree. How were they able to avoid competition and co-exist?
    (iii) State two important roles of predators in nature, with an example of each.
    (iv) What is brood parasitism? Give an example and state one adaptation shown by the parasite.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (i) $\dfrac{dN}{dt} = rN$. The curve is J-shaped.
    (ii) $\dfrac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(\dfrac{K-N}{K}\right)$. The curve is sigmoid (S-shaped). K is the carrying capacity: the maximum population size that the resources of the habitat can support.
    (iii) $\dfrac{dN}{dt} = 0.2 \times 200 \times \dfrac{1000 - 200}{1000} = 40 \times 0.8 = 32$ mice per year.
    (iv) No population can grow exponentially for ever, because the resources of a habitat are finite and sooner or later become limiting. The logistic model takes this into account.

    Marking scheme:

    • $dN/dt = rN$ (½ marks)
    • J-shaped curve (½ marks)
    • $dN/dt = rN(K-N)/K$ (½ marks)
    • Sigmoid (S-shaped) curve (½ marks)
    • K: carrying capacity, the maximum population the habitat's resources can support (1 mark)
    • Correct substitution: $0.2 \times 200 \times 800/1000$ (½ marks)
    • 32 mice per year (½ marks)
    • Resources are finite and become limiting (1 mark)

    OR (the other choice)

    Model answer: (i) Two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot co-exist indefinitely; the competitively inferior one will be eliminated eventually.
    (ii) By resource partitioning: the warblers showed behavioural differences in their foraging activities (they fed in different parts of the tree), so they did not compete directly.
    (iii) (1) Predators keep prey populations under control. For example, the prickly pear cactus introduced into Australia spread rapidly and was brought under control only after a cactus-feeding predator (a moth) was brought in from its natural habitat. (2) Predators help to maintain species diversity by reducing the intensity of competition among competing prey species. For example, when the starfish Pisaster was removed from an intertidal community, more than 10 species of invertebrates became extinct within a year because of competition. (Predators also transfer energy across trophic levels.)
    (iv) In brood parasitism, the parasitic bird lays its eggs in the nest of its host and lets the host incubate them. Example: the cuckoo (koel) lays its eggs in the nest of the crow. Adaptation: the eggs of the parasite resemble the host's eggs in size and colour, which reduces the chance of the host detecting and ejecting them.

    Marking scheme:

    • Gause's principle correctly stated (1 mark)
    • Resource partitioning: different foraging behaviour on the tree (1 mark)
    • Keep prey populations under control, with example (prickly pear in Australia) (1 mark)
    • Maintain species diversity by reducing competition among prey, with example (Pisaster), or transfer of energy (1 mark)
    • Brood parasitism: eggs laid in the host's nest, e.g. cuckoo in crow's nest (½ marks)
    • Eggs resemble the host's eggs in size and colour (½ marks)
