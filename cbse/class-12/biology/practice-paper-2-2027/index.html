Section A: 1 mark each

  1. Question 1 · 1 mark · Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants
    In a flowering plant, each cell of the nucellus has 16 chromosomes. How many chromosomes will each cell of its endosperm have?

    1. 8
    2. 16
    3. 24
    4. 32
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) 24

    The nucellus is a diploid tissue, so 2n = 16 and n = 8. The endosperm is triploid (3n), formed by fusion of a male gamete (n) with two polar nuclei (n + n), so it has 3 $\times$ 8 = 24 chromosomes.

  2. Question 2 · 1 mark · Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants
    Which pair of features is most likely to be found in a wind-pollinated flower?

    1. Large, brightly coloured petals and nectar
    2. Light, non-sticky pollen grains and a large, feathery stigma
    3. Strong fragrance and sticky pollen grains
    4. Few pollen grains and a small, smooth stigma
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) Light, non-sticky pollen grains and a large, feathery stigma

    Wind-pollinated flowers produce light, non-sticky pollen that the wind can carry, and have well-exposed, often feathery stigmas to trap the pollen. Bright petals, nectar and fragrance attract animal pollinators.

  3. Question 3 · 1 mark · Human Reproduction
    In a man, the Leydig cells of the testes are damaged. Which function will be directly affected?

    1. Secretion of androgens
    2. Nourishment of the developing germ cells
    3. Storage of sperms
    4. Secretion of seminal plasma
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) Secretion of androgens

    Leydig (interstitial) cells, present outside the seminiferous tubules, synthesise and secrete testicular hormones called androgens. Sertoli cells nourish germ cells, the epididymis stores sperms, and the accessory glands secrete seminal plasma.

  4. Question 4 · 1 mark · Principles of Inheritance and Variation
    A woman with normal vision, whose father was colour blind, marries a man with normal vision. What percentage of their sons are expected to be colour blind?

    1. 0%
    2. 25%
    3. 50%
    4. 100%
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) 50%

    The woman received the X chromosome carrying the colour blindness allele from her father, so she is a carrier ($X^cX$). Sons receive their X chromosome from the mother: half get $X^c$ (colour blind) and half get $X$ (normal). So 50% of the sons are expected to be colour blind.

  5. Question 5 · 1 mark · Principles of Inheritance and Variation
    A person has a total of 47 chromosomes with the sex chromosomes XXY. The person is most likely to have:

    1. Turner's syndrome
    2. Down's syndrome
    3. Phenylketonuria
    4. Klinefelter's syndrome
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) Klinefelter's syndrome

    Klinefelter's syndrome is caused by an extra X chromosome in a male, giving 47 chromosomes (44 + XXY). Turner's syndrome is 45, XO; Down's syndrome is trisomy 21; phenylketonuria is a Mendelian (single gene) disorder.

  6. Question 6 · 1 mark · Molecular Basis of Inheritance
    In the Hershey-Chase experiment, bacteria infected with bacteriophages grown on a medium containing radioactive phosphorus were radioactive after blending and centrifugation, while bacteria infected with phages grown on radioactive sulphur were not. What does this show?

    1. The protein coat of the phage enters the bacterium and is the genetic material.
    2. DNA of the phage enters the bacterium and is the genetic material.
    3. Both DNA and protein of the phage enter the bacterium equally.
    4. Radioactive sulphur kills the bacteria before infection.
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) DNA of the phage enters the bacterium and is the genetic material.

    Radioactive phosphorus labels DNA and radioactive sulphur labels protein. Only the phosphorus label was found inside the bacteria, so it is the DNA of the phage that enters the bacterium and passes on the genetic information.

  7. Question 7 · 1 mark · Evolution
    A number of marsupials, each different from the other, such as the kangaroo, koala and numbat, evolved in Australia from a common ancestral stock. This is an example of:

    1. convergent evolution
    2. industrial melanism
    3. genetic drift
    4. adaptive radiation
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) adaptive radiation

    Evolution of different species in a given geographical area, starting from one point and radiating to other habitats, is adaptive radiation. Australian marsupials arose this way from an ancestral stock within the island continent.

  8. Question 8 · 1 mark · Human Health and Disease
    A child in a village with poor sanitation has internal bleeding, muscular pain, fever, anaemia and blockage of the intestinal passage. The disease is spread by eating vegetables and drinking water contaminated with eggs of the parasite. The disease is most likely:

    1. filariasis
    2. amoebiasis
    3. typhoid
    4. ascariasis
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) ascariasis

    Ascariasis is caused by the intestinal parasite Ascaris, a helminth. Its eggs pass out in the faeces of infected persons and contaminate soil, water and plants; the symptoms listed are those of ascariasis.

  9. Question 9 · 1 mark · Human Health and Disease
    Which of the following is shown by malignant tumour cells but NOT by normal cells?

    1. Contact inhibition
    2. Controlled cell division
    3. Metastasis
    4. Normal differentiation into specialised cells
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) Metastasis

    Cancer cells lose contact inhibition and divide in an uncontrolled way. Malignant tumour cells can travel through the blood to distant sites and start new tumours there; this property is called metastasis. The other three are properties of normal cells.

  10. Question 10 · 1 mark · Microbes in Human Welfare
    Which of the following correctly matches a microbe with the product made from it on an industrial scale?

    1. Aspergillus niger - citric acid
    2. Acetobacter aceti - lactic acid
    3. Clostridium butylicum - ethanol
    4. Lactobacillus - acetic acid
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) Aspergillus niger - citric acid

    Aspergillus niger (a fungus) produces citric acid. Acetobacter aceti produces acetic acid, Clostridium butylicum produces butyric acid and Lactobacillus produces lactic acid.

  11. Question 11 · 1 mark · Biotechnology: Principles and Processes
    A circular plasmid has only one recognition site for EcoR I. If the plasmid is completely digested with EcoR I, how many DNA fragments will be obtained?

    1. One
    2. Two
    3. Three
    4. None, as circular DNA cannot be cut
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) One

    Cutting a circular DNA at one site opens the circle into a single linear fragment (with sticky ends at both ends). A linear DNA cut at one site would give two fragments.

  12. Question 12 · 1 mark · Organisms and Populations
    A pond has a population of 200 frogs. During a year, 20 new frogs are added by births and 10 frogs die; there is no immigration or emigration. What is the birth rate of this population?

    1. 0.05 per frog per year
    2. 0.1 per frog per year
    3. 0.15 per frog per year
    4. 0.2 per frog per year
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) 0.1 per frog per year

    Birth rate = number of births per individual per unit time = 20 $\div$ 200 = 0.1 per frog per year. (The death rate would be 10 $\div$ 200 = 0.05.)

  13. Question 13 · 1 mark · Reproductive Health
    Assertion (A): There is a statutory ban on amniocentesis for sex determination in India.
    Reason (R): Amniocentesis can detect certain genetic disorders by analysing foetal cells in the amniotic fluid.

    1. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
    2. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
    3. A is true but R is false.
    4. A is false but R is true.
    Show answer

    Answer: (b) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.

    Both statements are true. The ban exists because amniocentesis was being misused for sex determination, leading to female foeticide. Its use for detecting genetic disorders (R) is true but does not explain the ban, so R is not the correct explanation of A.

  14. Question 14 · 1 mark · Molecular Basis of Inheritance
    Assertion (A): In eukaryotes, the RNA first transcribed from a split gene cannot be used directly for translation.
    Reason (R): The primary transcript contains introns, which are removed by splicing; it also undergoes capping and tailing before it becomes functional mRNA.

    1. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
    2. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
    3. A is true but R is false.
    4. A is false but R is true.
    Show answer

    Answer: (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.

    Both are true. The primary transcript (hnRNA) has both exons and introns and is non-functional. It must be spliced (introns removed) and given a 5' cap and a 3' poly-A tail before it is transported out of the nucleus for translation. So R correctly explains A.

  15. Question 15 · 1 mark · Biotechnology and its Applications
    Assertion (A): To make human insulin by recombinant DNA technology, two DNA sequences coding for the A and B chains were introduced separately into plasmids of E. coli.
    Reason (R): Mature, functional human insulin contains the C-peptide, which links the A and B chains.

    1. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
    2. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
    3. A is true but R is false.
    4. A is false but R is true.
    Show answer

    Answer: (c) A is true but R is false.

    A is true: the A and B chains were produced separately in E. coli, extracted and combined by forming disulphide bonds. R is false: the C-peptide is present only in pro-insulin and is removed during maturation, so mature insulin does not contain it.

  16. Question 16 · 1 mark · Biodiversity and Conservation
    Assertion (A): Introduction of alien species into a region always increases its biodiversity.
    Reason (R): Introduction of the Nile perch into Lake Victoria led to the extinction of more than 200 species of cichlid fish.

    1. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
    2. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A.
    3. A is true but R is false.
    4. A is false but R is true.
    Show answer

    Answer: (d) A is false but R is true.

    A is false: alien species often become invasive and cause decline or extinction of native species, reducing biodiversity. R is true and is an example of such a loss.

Section B: 2 marks each

  1. Question 17 · 2 marks · Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants
    A fruit seller has seedless bananas, and an orange in which some seeds contain more than one embryo. Name the phenomenon seen in each fruit and explain how it occurs.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: Seedless banana: parthenocarpy. The fruit develops without fertilisation, so no seeds are formed (it can also be induced by applying growth hormones).
    Orange: polyembryony. Some nucellar cells surrounding the embryo sac start dividing, protrude into the embryo sac and develop into embryos, so one seed has many embryos (along with the zygotic embryo).

    Marking scheme:

    • Banana: parthenocarpy (0.5); fruit develops without fertilisation (0.5) (1 mark)
    • Orange: polyembryony (0.5); nucellar cells divide and develop into additional embryos (0.5) (1 mark)

  2. Question 18 · 2 marks · Human Reproduction
    How many sperms are formed from 25 primary spermatocytes, and how many ova are formed from 25 primary oocytes? Explain the reason for the difference.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: 25 primary spermatocytes form 25 $\times$ 4 = 100 sperms. 25 primary oocytes form only 25 ova.
    Reason: in spermatogenesis, each primary spermatocyte undergoes meiosis to form four spermatids, all of which become sperms. In oogenesis, both meiotic divisions are unequal: each forms one large cell and one small polar body. The polar bodies degenerate, so each primary oocyte gives only one ovum.

    Marking scheme:

    • 100 sperms (½ marks)
    • 25 ova (½ marks)
    • Reason: unequal meiotic divisions in oogenesis form polar bodies that degenerate, so one ovum per primary oocyte, while one primary spermatocyte gives four sperms (1 mark)

  3. Question 19 · 2 marks · Human Health and Disease
    (a) Explain the principle of vaccination.
    (b) How has recombinant DNA technology helped in the production of vaccines? Give one example.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Vaccination is based on the 'memory' of the immune system. A preparation of antigenic proteins of a pathogen, or inactivated or weakened pathogens, is introduced into the body. The body produces antibodies against the antigens and also forms memory B and T cells. On later exposure to the same pathogen, the memory cells recognise it quickly and produce a large, rapid response that destroys it.
    (b) Recombinant DNA technology allows antigenic polypeptides of a pathogen to be produced in bacteria or yeast on a large scale, making vaccines available in large amounts. Example: hepatitis B vaccine produced from yeast.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Antigen (weakened or inactivated pathogen / antigenic protein) induces antibodies and memory B and T cells (0.5); quick, strong response on later exposure (0.5) (1 mark)
    • (b) Antigenic polypeptides produced on a large scale in bacteria or yeast (0.5); example: hepatitis B vaccine from yeast (0.5) (1 mark)

  4. Question 20 · 2 marks · Microbes in Human Welfare
    In a sewage treatment plant, the primary effluent is passed into an aeration tank, and samples are tested regularly for BOD.
    (a) What does a large fall in the BOD of the effluent in the aeration tank show?
    (b) What happens to the activated sludge after the effluent is passed into the settling tank?

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) A large fall in BOD shows that most of the organic matter in the effluent has been consumed (oxidised) by the aerobic microbes that form flocs in the aeration tank.
    (b) The bacterial flocs settle down as activated sludge. A small part of it is pumped back into the aeration tank as an inoculum. The major part is pumped into large anaerobic sludge digesters, where anaerobic bacteria digest the bacteria and fungi in the sludge, producing biogas.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Organic matter has been consumed by aerobic microbes (flocs), so the effluent is now low in organic matter (1 mark)
    • (b) Part of the sludge is returned to the aeration tank as inoculum (0.5); the rest goes to anaerobic sludge digesters where it is digested, producing biogas (0.5) (1 mark)

  5. Question 21 · 2 marks · Human Health and Disease
    A teenager is found to be using 'smack'.
    (a) Name the chemical present in smack and state how it is obtained.
    (b) State the effect of this drug on body functions.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Smack is heroin, chemically diacetylmorphine. It is obtained by acetylation of morphine, which is extracted from the latex of the poppy plant Papaver somniferum.
    (b) Heroin is a depressant: it binds to opioid receptors in the central nervous system and gastrointestinal tract and slows down body functions.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Heroin / diacetylmorphine (0.5); obtained by acetylation of morphine from the latex of poppy, Papaver somniferum (0.5) (1 mark)
    • (b) Depressant: slows down body functions (acts on opioid receptors in the CNS and gut) (1 mark)

Section C: 3 marks each

  1. Question 22 · 3 marks · Reproductive Health
    Name the assisted reproductive technology suitable for each of the following couples. Give a reason in each case.
    (a) The woman cannot produce ova but can provide a suitable environment for fertilisation and development.
    (b) The male partner produces normal semen but is unable to inseminate the female.
    (c) Fertilisation can be achieved in the laboratory, and the doctor wants to transfer the early embryo with up to 8 blastomeres into the woman's reproductive tract.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) GIFT (gamete intra fallopian transfer): an ovum collected from a donor is transferred into the fallopian tube of the woman, where fertilisation can take place naturally.
    (b) Artificial insemination (AI) / intra-uterine insemination (IUI): semen collected from the husband (or a donor) is artificially introduced into the vagina or the uterus of the woman.
    (c) ZIFT (zygote intra fallopian transfer): the zygote or early embryo (up to 8 blastomeres), formed by in vitro fertilisation, is transferred into the fallopian tube. (Embryos with more than 8 blastomeres are transferred into the uterus: IUT.)

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) GIFT (0.5); donor ovum transferred into the fallopian tube (0.5) (1 mark)
    • (b) Artificial insemination / IUI (0.5); semen introduced artificially into the vagina or uterus (0.5) (1 mark)
    • (c) ZIFT (0.5); zygote or embryo up to 8 blastomeres transferred into the fallopian tube (0.5) (1 mark)

  2. Question 23 · 3 marks · Molecular Basis of Inheritance
    In a disputed parentage case, DNA fingerprints of a mother, her child and two men (X and Y) were prepared. The positions of the bands on the gel (numbered from the top) are given below.

    IndividualBand positions
    Mother1, 4, 6
    Child1, 3, 6, 8
    Man X2, 3, 5, 8
    Man Y3, 4, 7
    (a) Which man is likely to be the biological father of the child? Justify your answer.
    (b) What are VNTRs, and why are they useful for DNA fingerprinting?
    (c) Name the technique used to transfer the separated DNA fragments from the gel to a nylon or nitrocellulose membrane.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Man X. Every band of the child must come from either the mother or the father. Bands 1 and 6 of the child are present in the mother; the remaining bands 3 and 8 must come from the father. Man X has both bands 3 and 8, while man Y has band 3 but not band 8.
    (b) VNTRs (variable number tandem repeats) are short DNA sequences repeated one after another (in tandem) many times. The number of copies varies from person to person (high polymorphism), so the size of the fragments differs and every individual (except identical twins) has a unique pattern of bands.
    (c) Southern blotting.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Man X (0.5); child's non-maternal bands 3 and 8 are both present only in X (0.5) (1 mark)
    • (b) VNTRs are tandemly repeated short sequences (0.5); copy number varies between individuals, giving each person a unique band pattern (0.5) (1 mark)
    • (c) Southern blotting (1 mark)

  3. Question 24 · 3 marks · Evolution
    In 1953, S.L. Miller passed an electric discharge through a closed flask containing $\mathrm{CH_4}$, $\mathrm{H_2}$, $\mathrm{NH_3}$ and water vapour at 800 °C.
    (a) What did Miller observe at the end of the experiment?
    (b) What did the conditions inside the flask represent?
    (c) Which theory of the origin of life did this experiment support? State the theory briefly.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Formation of amino acids (simple organic molecules) from the inorganic gases.
    (b) The conditions on the primitive (early) earth: high temperature, lightning (electric discharge) and an atmosphere of methane, hydrogen, ammonia and water vapour.
    (c) The theory of chemical evolution proposed by Oparin and Haldane: the first form of life came from pre-existing non-living organic molecules (such as RNA and proteins), and the formation of life was preceded by chemical evolution, i.e. formation of diverse organic molecules from inorganic constituents.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Amino acids were formed (1 mark)
    • (b) Conditions of the primitive earth (high temperature, electric discharge as lightning, reducing gases) (1 mark)
    • (c) Oparin-Haldane theory of chemical evolution (0.5); life arose from non-living organic molecules formed from inorganic constituents (0.5) (1 mark)

  4. Question 25 · 3 marks · Microbes in Human Welfare
    (a) Describe how penicillin, the first antibiotic, was discovered.
    (b) Why are doctors advised to prescribe antibiotics only when they are really needed and for the full course?
    (c) Why is an antibiotic of no use in treating the common cold?

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Alexander Fleming, while working on Staphylococci bacteria, noticed a mould growing on one of his unwashed culture plates, around which the bacteria could not grow. He found that this was due to a chemical produced by the mould and named it penicillin, after the mould Penicillium notatum. (Its full potential as an antibiotic was later established by Chain and Florey.)
    (b) Excessive or improper use of antibiotics leads to the selection of antibiotic-resistant bacteria; these multiply and the antibiotic stops working against them.
    (c) The common cold is caused by viruses (rhino viruses). Antibiotics act against bacteria and have no effect on viruses.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Fleming observed that Staphylococci did not grow around the mould Penicillium notatum; the chemical was named penicillin (1 mark)
    • (b) Misuse / overuse selects for antibiotic-resistant bacteria (1 mark)
    • (c) Common cold is caused by viruses (rhino viruses), and antibiotics do not act on viruses (1 mark)

  5. Question 26 · 3 marks · Biotechnology: Principles and Processes
    A scientist wants to introduce a recombinant DNA into different host cells.
    (a) Which bacterium has been modified to deliver genes into plant cells? How has it been made safe?
    (b) Name the methods by which recombinant DNA can be introduced, without a vector, into (i) an animal cell and (ii) a plant cell.
    (c) Why are bacterial cells treated with calcium ions before transformation, and what is done next to force the DNA into them?

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Agrobacterium tumefaciens, a pathogen of dicot plants. Its tumour-inducing (Ti) plasmid has been modified so that it is no longer pathogenic but can still deliver genes of interest into plant cells.
    (b) (i) Microinjection: the recombinant DNA is injected directly into the nucleus of the animal cell. (ii) Biolistics (gene gun): plant cells are bombarded with high-velocity micro-particles of gold or tungsten coated with DNA.
    (c) DNA is a hydrophilic molecule and cannot pass through cell membranes. Treatment with a divalent cation such as calcium makes the cells competent, increasing the efficiency with which DNA enters through pores in the cell wall. The cells are then incubated with the recombinant DNA on ice, given a heat shock (briefly placed at 42 °C) and put back on ice.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Agrobacterium tumefaciens / Ti plasmid (0.5); modified to be non-pathogenic while still delivering genes (0.5) (1 mark)
    • (b) (i) Microinjection into the nucleus (0.5); (ii) biolistics / gene gun (0.5) (1 mark)
    • (c) Calcium makes cells competent so DNA enters more efficiently (0.5); incubation on ice, heat shock at 42 °C, then back on ice (0.5) (1 mark)

  6. Question 27 · 3 marks · Biotechnology and its Applications
    In 1997, an American company got patent rights on a 'new' variety of Basmati rice from the US Patent and Trademark Office.
    (a) What is biopiracy?
    (b) Why was this patent objected to by India?
    (c) Name the organisation set up by the Government of India that decides on the validity of GM research and the safety of introducing GM organisms for public services.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Biopiracy is the use of bio-resources (and traditional knowledge) by multinational companies and other organisations without proper authorisation from the countries and people concerned and without compensatory payment.
    (b) The 'new' variety had actually been derived from Indian farmers' varieties: Indian Basmati was crossed with semi-dwarf varieties and claimed as an invention. This allowed the company to sell it in the US and abroad, using a resource developed by Indian farmers without benefit to them.
    (c) GEAC (Genetic Engineering Approval Committee).

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Use of bio-resources by companies/organisations without authorisation and without compensatory payment (1 mark)
    • (b) The variety was derived from Indian farmers' Basmati varieties (crossed with semi-dwarf varieties) and wrongly claimed as a novelty (1 mark)
    • (c) GEAC (Genetic Engineering Approval Committee) (1 mark)

  7. Question 28 · 3 marks · Biodiversity and Conservation
    Paul Ehrlich compared an ecosystem to an aeroplane and its species to the rivets that hold the aeroplane together.
    (a) Explain this 'rivet popper' hypothesis.
    (b) What would be the effect of losing rivets on the main wings, compared with losing rivets on the seats or windows? Relate this to species in an ecosystem.
    (c) State one reason, other than ecosystem stability, why we should conserve biodiversity.

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    Model answer: (a) Every species is like a rivet in an aeroplane (the ecosystem). If rivets are removed one by one (species become extinct), it may not affect flight safety at first, but as more and more rivets are lost, the plane becomes dangerously weak; similarly, the ecosystem becomes weaker as species are lost.
    (b) Losing rivets on the wings (key species that drive major ecosystem functions) is a far more serious threat to flight safety than losing rivets on seats or windows. Loss of key species can damage the ecosystem much more than loss of other species.
    (c) Any one: narrowly utilitarian - humans get direct economic benefits such as food, firewood, fibre, medicines and industrial products; broadly utilitarian - ecosystem services such as oxygen production, pollination of crops and flood control; ethical - every species has an intrinsic value and we have a moral duty to pass it on to future generations.

    Marking scheme:

    • (a) Species are like rivets: losing species one by one gradually weakens the ecosystem until it may fail (1 mark)
    • (b) Loss of key species (wing rivets) that drive major functions is much more damaging than loss of other species (1 mark)
    • (c) Any one correct reason: narrowly utilitarian (direct products), broadly utilitarian (ecosystem services) or ethical (1 mark)

Section D: case-based, 4 marks each

  1. Question 29 · 4 marks · Evolution
    On an island, the shell colour of a land snail is controlled by one gene with two alleles: B (brown) is dominant over b (yellow). A survey of 1000 snails found 90 yellow snails. Assume that the population is in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium.

    (i) Calculate the frequency of allele b in the population. [1]

    (ii) Calculate the expected number of heterozygous brown snails and of homozygous brown snails in this population. Show your working. [2]

    (iii) A storm carries a few snails to a nearby empty island. In the new population, yellow snails become the most common, purely by chance. Name this effect. [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    (i) Calculate the frequency of allele b in the population.

    Model answer: Frequency of yellow snails (bb) = $q^2 = \frac{90}{1000} = 0.09$, so $q = \sqrt{0.09} = 0.3$.

    Marking scheme:

    • $q^2 = 0.09$ (½ marks)
    • $q = 0.3$ (½ marks)

    (ii) Calculate the expected number of heterozygous brown snails and of homozygous brown snails in this population. Show your working.

    Model answer: $p = 1 - q = 1 - 0.3 = 0.7$.
    Heterozygous (Bb): $2pq = 2 \times 0.7 \times 0.3 = 0.42$, so $0.42 \times 1000 = 420$ snails.
    Homozygous brown (BB): $p^2 = 0.7^2 = 0.49$, so $0.49 \times 1000 = 490$ snails.
    (Check: 490 + 420 + 90 = 1000.)

    Marking scheme:

    • $p = 0.7$ and correct use of $2pq$ (½ marks)
    • Heterozygous = 420 snails (½ marks)
    • Correct use of $p^2 = 0.49$ (½ marks)
    • Homozygous brown = 490 snails (½ marks)

    (iii) A storm carries a few snails to a nearby empty island. In the new population, yellow snails become the most common, purely by chance. Name this effect.

    Model answer: Founder effect, a type of genetic drift: a change in allele frequency that occurs by chance in a small population that has moved to a new place; the original drifted population becomes the founder.

    Marking scheme:

    • Founder effect / genetic drift (1 mark)

  2. Question 30 · 4 marks · Biotechnology and its Applications
    Early detection of a disease helps in its effective treatment. By conventional methods such as serum and urine analysis, a disease can usually be detected only when the pathogen has multiplied and symptoms have appeared. Recombinant DNA technology, PCR and ELISA are now used for early diagnosis. A clinic tests a person who was exposed to HIV a few weeks ago, before any symptoms appear.

    (i) Why can PCR detect a pathogen even when it is present in very low numbers? [1]

    (ii) State the principle of ELISA and name the two kinds of molecules it can detect to diagnose an infection. [2]

    (iii) Besides detecting HIV in suspected AIDS patients, state one other use of PCR in molecular diagnosis. [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    (i) Why can PCR detect a pathogen even when it is present in very low numbers?

    Model answer: PCR amplifies (makes millions of copies of) the nucleic acid of the pathogen, so even a very small amount of the pathogen's DNA or RNA becomes enough to detect.

    Marking scheme:

    • PCR amplifies the pathogen's nucleic acid, so a tiny amount becomes detectable (1 mark)

    (ii) State the principle of ELISA and name the two kinds of molecules it can detect to diagnose an infection.

    Model answer: ELISA (enzyme linked immuno-sorbent assay) is based on the principle of antigen-antibody interaction. Infection by a pathogen can be detected by the presence of (1) antigens of the pathogen (proteins, glycoproteins, etc.) or (2) antibodies synthesised by the body against the pathogen.

    Marking scheme:

    • Principle: antigen-antibody interaction (1 mark)
    • Detects antigens of the pathogen (proteins, glycoproteins) (½ marks)
    • Detects antibodies made against the pathogen (½ marks)

    (iii) Besides detecting HIV in suspected AIDS patients, state one other use of PCR in molecular diagnosis.

    Model answer: PCR is used to detect mutations in genes in suspected cancer patients (it can also identify many other genetic disorders).

    Marking scheme:

    • Detecting mutations in genes of suspected cancer patients (or other genetic disorders) (1 mark)

Section E: 5 marks each

  1. Question 31 · 5 marks · Human Reproduction
    After ovulation, the ovum of a woman meets sperms in her fallopian tube. Answer the following about the events from fertilisation to implantation.

    (i) In which part of the fallopian tube does fertilisation take place, and how is the entry of more than one sperm into the ovum prevented? [1]

    (ii) What change takes place in the secondary oocyte when the sperm enters it? [1]

    (iii) Describe the stages through which the zygote passes as it moves towards the uterus, up to the formation of the blastocyst. [2]

    (iv) Explain implantation. [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    (i) In which part of the fallopian tube does fertilisation take place, and how is the entry of more than one sperm into the ovum prevented?

    Model answer: Fertilisation takes place in the ampullary region (ampulla) of the fallopian tube. When a sperm comes in contact with the zona pellucida of the ovum, it induces changes in the membrane that block the entry of additional sperms.

    Marking scheme:

    • Ampullary region (ampulla) (½ marks)
    • Sperm contact with the zona pellucida causes membrane changes that block other sperms (½ marks)

    (ii) What change takes place in the secondary oocyte when the sperm enters it?

    Model answer: The entry of the sperm induces the secondary oocyte to complete its second meiotic division. This division is unequal and forms a second polar body and a haploid ovum (ootid). The haploid nuclei of sperm and ovum then fuse to form the diploid zygote.

    Marking scheme:

    • Completion of meiosis II (½ marks)
    • Second polar body and haploid ootid formed (then fusion gives a diploid zygote) (½ marks)

    (iii) Describe the stages through which the zygote passes as it moves towards the uterus, up to the formation of the blastocyst.

    Model answer: As the zygote moves through the isthmus towards the uterus, it undergoes mitotic divisions called cleavage and forms 2, 4, 8, 16 daughter cells called blastomeres. The embryo with 8 to 16 blastomeres is called a morula. The morula continues to divide and changes into a blastocyst as it moves further into the uterus. In the blastocyst, the blastomeres are arranged into an outer layer called the trophoblast and an inner group of cells attached to it, called the inner cell mass.

    Marking scheme:

    • Cleavage (mitotic divisions) forming blastomeres (½ marks)
    • Morula: 8 to 16 blastomeres (½ marks)
    • Morula changes into blastocyst (½ marks)
    • Blastocyst has outer trophoblast and inner cell mass (½ marks)

    (iv) Explain implantation.

    Model answer: The trophoblast layer of the blastocyst gets attached to the endometrium. The uterine cells then divide rapidly and cover the blastocyst, so the blastocyst becomes embedded in the endometrium of the uterus. This is implantation; the inner cell mass then differentiates into the embryo.

    Marking scheme:

    • Trophoblast attaches to the endometrium (½ marks)
    • Uterine cells divide and cover the blastocyst, which becomes embedded in the endometrium (½ marks)

  2. Question 32 · 5 marks · Principles of Inheritance and Variation
    T.H. Morgan carried out several dihybrid crosses in Drosophila to study genes that are sex-linked. In one cross, the genes for yellow body and white eyes gave only 1.3 per cent recombinants. In another cross, the genes for white eyes and miniature wings gave 37.2 per cent recombinants.

    (i) Give two reasons why Drosophila is suitable for genetic studies. [1]

    (ii) Which pair of genes is more tightly linked? Justify your answer. [1]

    (iii) Explain why the $\mathrm{F_2}$ ratio in such crosses deviated greatly from the 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio expected by Mendel. [1]

    (iv) How did Alfred Sturtevant use such recombination frequencies? [1]

    (v) Name the type of sex determination in Drosophila and state which sex is heterogametic. [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    (i) Give two reasons why Drosophila is suitable for genetic studies.

    Model answer: Any two: it completes its life cycle in about two weeks; a single mating produces a large number of progeny flies; there is a clear differentiation of the sexes (male and female flies are easily distinguished); it has many types of hereditary variations that can be seen with low-power microscopes; it can be grown on a simple synthetic medium in the laboratory.

    Marking scheme:

    • Any one correct reason (½ marks)
    • Any second correct reason (½ marks)

    (ii) Which pair of genes is more tightly linked? Justify your answer.

    Model answer: Yellow body and white eyes. They show a much lower frequency of recombination (1.3%) than white eyes and miniature wings (37.2%); the lower the recombination frequency, the stronger the linkage (the genes lie closer together).

    Marking scheme:

    • Yellow body and white eyes (½ marks)
    • Lower recombination (1.3%) means stronger linkage / genes closer together (½ marks)

    (iii) Explain why the $\mathrm{F_2}$ ratio in such crosses deviated greatly from the 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio expected by Mendel.

    Model answer: The two genes were located on the same chromosome (the X chromosome), so they did not assort independently. Linked genes tend to be inherited together, giving many more parental combinations and far fewer recombinants than expected.

    Marking scheme:

    • Genes are on the same (X) chromosome / linked, so they do not assort independently (½ marks)
    • Parental combinations are much more frequent than recombinants (½ marks)

    (iv) How did Alfred Sturtevant use such recombination frequencies?

    Model answer: Sturtevant used the frequency of recombination between gene pairs on the same chromosome as a measure of the distance between the genes, and used it to map the positions of genes on the chromosome (genetic maps).

    Marking scheme:

    • Recombination frequency used as a measure of distance between genes (½ marks)
    • To map the positions of genes on the chromosome (½ marks)

    (v) Name the type of sex determination in Drosophila and state which sex is heterogametic.

    Model answer: XX-XY type (male heterogamety). The males (XY) are heterogametic: they produce two kinds of sperms, with X or with Y; the females (XX) are homogametic.

    Marking scheme:

    • XY type (XX-XY) (½ marks)
    • Male is heterogametic (½ marks)

  3. Question 33 · 5 marks · Ecosystem
    In a pond ecosystem, the producers fix 5000 kJ $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ $\mathrm{year^{-1}}$ of energy as gross primary productivity (GPP) and use 2000 kJ $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ $\mathrm{year^{-1}}$ in respiration. The herbivores obtain 300 kJ $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ $\mathrm{year^{-1}}$ and the carnivores that eat them obtain 30 kJ $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ $\mathrm{year^{-1}}$ (hypothetical data).

    (i) Calculate the net primary productivity (NPP) of the pond. [1]

    (ii) What percentage of the NPP is passed on to the herbivores? Name the law that this illustrates. [1]

    (iii) Draw a labelled pyramid of energy for this pond with three trophic levels (use the NPP for the producers), and explain why a pyramid of energy is always upright. [2]

    (iv) In the sea, the pyramid of biomass is generally inverted, but the pyramid of energy is not. Explain why the pyramid of biomass is inverted. [1]

    Show model answer and marking scheme

    (i) Calculate the net primary productivity (NPP) of the pond.

    Model answer: NPP = GPP $-$ R = 5000 $-$ 2000 = 3000 kJ $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ $\mathrm{year^{-1}}$.

    Marking scheme:

    • NPP = GPP $-$ R (½ marks)
    • 3000 kJ $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ $\mathrm{year^{-1}}$ (½ marks)

    (ii) What percentage of the NPP is passed on to the herbivores? Name the law that this illustrates.

    Model answer: $\frac{300}{3000} \times 100 = 10\%$. This illustrates the 10 per cent law (only about 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next).

    Marking scheme:

    • 10% (½ marks)
    • 10 per cent law (½ marks)

    (iii) Draw a labelled pyramid of energy for this pond with three trophic levels (use the NPP for the producers), and explain why a pyramid of energy is always upright.

    Model answer: The pyramid has three bars, broadest at the base: producers (first trophic level) 3000 kJ $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ $\mathrm{year^{-1}}$ at the base; herbivores / primary consumers (second trophic level) 300; carnivores / secondary consumers (third trophic level) 30 at the top.
    It is always upright because energy flows in one direction and at each step some energy is lost as heat (in respiration and other activities); only about 10% passes to the next level, so each higher level has less energy than the one below.

    Marking scheme:

    • Pyramid drawn with base broadest, trophic levels labelled (producers, herbivores, carnivores) (½ marks)
    • Correct values 3000, 300 and 30 kJ $\mathrm{m^{-2}}$ $\mathrm{year^{-1}}$ (½ marks)
    • Explanation: energy is lost as heat at each step / only about 10% passes on, so energy decreases at each higher level (1 mark)

    (iv) In the sea, the pyramid of biomass is generally inverted, but the pyramid of energy is not. Explain why the pyramid of biomass is inverted.

    Model answer: In the sea, the biomass of fishes (consumers) at any time far exceeds that of the phytoplankton (producers), which are small, short-lived and multiply rapidly; so the producer level has the smallest standing biomass and the pyramid of biomass is inverted.

    Marking scheme:

    • Biomass of fishes (consumers) far exceeds that of phytoplankton (producers) at a given time (1 mark)
